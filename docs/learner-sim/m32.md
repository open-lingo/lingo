# m32 — Conditionals I — たら, and と as the contrast

Module 32 · JLPT N4

The most useful conditional in the language is also the cheapest: take the plain past you have made since module 11 and put ら on the end. たら means if AND when, and Japanese never makes you pick between them. Then the one form it has to be told apart from: と, which claims the result happens EVERY time — seasons, machines, and the directions somebody gives you in the street. The difference is not a feel. Look at the end of the sentence: if it is a request, an invitation or a plan, と is blocked and たら is the answer. Along the way you get the politest suggestion in the language, and how to say roughly when and roughly how long.

## Lesson 1 — 「あめが ふったら」 — the past form, plus ら (ja-m32-neo-1)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「あめが ふったら」 — the past form, plus ら
   たら is the cheapest thing in this whole level. Take the plain past — the た-form you have made since module 11 — and put ら on the end. たべた becomes たべたら, いった becomes いったら, ふった becomes ふったら. That is the entire construction: no new conjugation, nothing to memorise beyond "the past form, plus ら". It means "if/when X happens", and what follows is what happens next. The verb at the END of the sentence carries the tense, exactly as it did for まえに — たら itself never says when.
      · あめが ふったら、いえに いる。  —  If it rains, I'll stay home.
      · えきに ついたら、でんわを かける。  —  When I get to the station, I'll call.
      · ごはんを たべたら、ねる。  —  When I've eaten, I'll sleep.
      · おかねが あったら、くるまを かう。  —  If I had money, I'd buy a car.
      · うちに かえったら、しゅくだいを する。  —  When I get home, I'll do my homework.
   NOT: あめが ふるたら、いえに いる。 — ら goes on the PAST form, never the dictionary form — ふる becomes ふった first: あめが ふったら、いえに いる。

## Lesson 2 — 「はるに なったら」 — if and when are the same word (ja-m32-neo-2)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「はるに なったら」 — if and when are the same word
   English makes you choose. "If it rains" doubts the rain; "when it rains" expects it. Japanese does not make you choose, and たら is the same word for both. 「あめが ふったら」 is if-or-when, and the sentence never says which — the listener works it out from whether the thing is likely. That is not Japanese being vague. It is Japanese leaving out a distinction English is forced to carry, the same way it leaves out the difference between one book and some books. Do not go looking for a second form for "when"; たら is it.
      · はるに なったら、はなが さく。  —  When spring comes, the flowers bloom.
      · あした あめが ふったら、いかない。  —  If it rains tomorrow, I won't go.
      · だいがくに はいったら、にほんごを ならう。  —  When I get into university, I'll study Japanese.
      · じかんが あったら、あそぶ。  —  If I have time, I'll play.
      · うちに かえったら、ほんを よむ。  —  When I get home, I'll read a book.

## Lesson 3 — 「ついたら、でんわを して ください」 — たら takes any ending (ja-m32-neo-3)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「ついたら、でんわを して ください」 — たら takes any ending
   Here is what たら can do that と cannot, and it is the practical half of this module. The second clause of a たら sentence may be anything at all — a request, an invitation, a want, a plan. 「えきに ついたら、でんわを して ください」. 「やすみに なったら、うみに いきたい」. Nothing is blocked, because たら only claims that one thing follows another and makes no claim that it always does. Whenever you are unsure which conditional to reach for, たら is the safe answer.
      · えきに ついたら、でんわを して ください。  —  When you get to the station, please call.
      · やすみに なったら、うみに いきたい。  —  When the holidays come, I want to go to the sea.
      · その ほんを よんだら、わたしに かして ください。  —  When you've read that book, please lend it to me.
      · じかんが あったら、あそびに いきましょう。  —  If you have time, let's go and hang out.
      · うちに かえったら、しゅくだいを して ください。  —  When you get home, please do your homework.

## Lesson 5 — 「ボタンを おすと」 — the result that never varies (ja-m32-neo-5)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「ボタンを おすと」 — the result that never varies
   と is the other conditional, and it says something stronger: EVERY TIME. Put the dictionary form in front of it and と claims the result is automatic — this is how the world works, not what you expect on this one occasion. Seasons are the clearest case: 「はるに なると、はなが さく」, and nobody has to decide whether the flowers feel like it. Anything mechanical behaves the same way, which is why you will meet と all through the sentences below on switches and engines. The form could not be simpler, because nothing changes at all: dictionary form, then と. What you are choosing when you reach for it is not a tense — it is a claim about the world.
      · はるに なると、はなが さく。  —  When spring comes, the flowers bloom.
      · ふゆに なると、ゆきが ふる。  —  When winter comes, it snows.
      · あきに なると、さむく なる。  —  When autumn comes, it gets cold.
      · なつに なると、あつく なる。  —  When summer comes, it gets hot.
      · はるに なると、あたたかく なる。  —  When spring comes, it gets warm.
   NOT: はるに なったと、はなが さく。 — と takes the DICTIONARY form, never the past — たら is the one that takes た: はるに なると、はなが さく。

## Lesson 7 — 「ついたら、でんわを して ください」 — what と cannot say (ja-m32-neo-7)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「ついたら、でんわを して ください」 — what と cannot say
   と has ONE restriction and it is the whole contrast lesson. Because と claims the result is automatic, the second half cannot be something you are asking for, suggesting, ordering or planning — those are all things that might not happen, and と has already promised that this one always does. ×「えきに つくと、でんわを して ください」 is broken as a shape. Swap in たら and it is fixed: 「えきに ついたら、でんわを して ください」. So the test is mechanical rather than a feel: look at the END of the sentence. If it is ください, ましょう, たい, つもり, or a plain command, you need たら.
      · えきに ついたら、でんわを して ください。  —  When you get to the station, please call.
      · この ボタンを おすと、ドアが うごく。  —  If you press this button, the door moves.
      · やすみに なったら、うみに いきましょう。  —  When the holidays come, let's go to the sea.
      · うちに かえったら、ねる つもりだ。  —  When I get home, I intend to sleep.
      · ふゆに なると、ゆきが ふる。  —  When winter comes, it snows.

## Lesson 9 — 「いしゃに いったら どうですか」 — suggesting without telling (ja-m32-neo-9)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「いしゃに いったら どうですか」 — suggesting without telling
   One ready-made phrase, and it is the politest way to suggest something in the language. Hang どうですか on a たら clause and you have "how about ~ing?": 「いしゃに いったら どうですか」. It is softer than a suggestion because it never actually says what you should do; it asks the other person what they make of the idea. 〜たら いい is the same shape making a recommendation instead of a question — 「はやく ねたら いい」, you'd do well to sleep early. Both are just たら with something on the end, so there is nothing new to build.
      · いしゃに いったら どうですか。  —  How about going to a doctor?
      · ちかてつに のったら どうですか。  —  How about taking the underground?
      · はやく ねたら いい。  —  You'd do well to sleep early.
      · せんせいに きいたら どうですか。  —  How about asking the teacher?
      · ちかみちを とおったら いい。  —  You'd do well to take the shortcut.

## Lesson 10 — 「さんじごろ」と「さんじかんぐらい」 — about, and around (ja-m32-neo-10)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「さんじごろ」と「じゅっぷんぐらい」 — about, and around
   Two suffixes that make a number approximate, and the only thing to get right is which one takes which kind of number. ごろ goes on a POINT in time — 「さんじごろ」, around three o'clock. ぐらい goes on an AMOUNT — 「じゅっぷんぐらい」, about ten minutes; 「せんえんぐらい」, about a thousand yen. A clock time takes ごろ, a quantity takes ぐらい, and the tell is whether you could ask "how long" or "how many" about it. ごろ also replaces に when it lands on a time — 「さんじごろ いく」, not 「さんじごろに いく」.
      · さんじごろ うちに かえる。  —  I'll go home around three.
      · えきまで じゅっぷんぐらい かかる。  —  It takes about ten minutes to the station.
      · この ほんは せんえんぐらいだ。  —  This book is about a thousand yen.
      · しちじごろ ごはんを たべる。  —  I eat around seven.
      · ちかてつに じゅっぷんぐらい のったら、うみに つく。  —  If you ride the underground for about ten minutes, you reach the sea.
   NOT: さんじぐらい うちに かえる。 — a point on the clock takes ごろ, not ぐらい — ぐらい is for amounts: さんじごろ うちに かえる。
