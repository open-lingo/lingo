# m22 — Body, health & help

Module 22 · Health

「あたまが いたい」 — the head is painful. Japanese makes the body part the SUBJECT, so が carries every symptom you will ever need, and 「わたしは あたまが いたい」 puts は and が to work in one clause. 「ねつが ある」 says a fever is simply there. Then the register turns: you tell a doctor 「あたまが いたいです」, ask a pharmacist 「くすりを ください」, hear 「おさけを のまないで ください」 and ask back 「のんでも いいですか」. いつも / よく / ときどき / あまり / ぜんぜん say how often, and 〜ほん counts anything longer than it is wide.

## Lesson 1 — 「あたまが いたい」 — the part that hurts takes が (ja-m22-neo-1)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「あたまが いたい」 — the part that hurts takes が
   English makes YOU the owner of a pain — I have a headache, my foot hurts. Japanese does the opposite: the body part is the thing that IS painful, and it takes が. 「あたまが いたい」 — the head is painful. That is the whole sentence, and it is already complete without a word for 'I', because the person who is talking is the person whose head it is. Two things follow. First, いたい is an ADJECTIVE, not a verb: it behaves exactly like たかい or おいしい, so it needs no だ after it and no を in front of it. Nothing is being done TO the head, so を is not a mistake of style, it is a sentence that does not parse. Second, the pattern is fixed and the only thing that changes is the noun: あたまが いたい, おなかが いたい, からだが いたい. Learn the frame once and every symptom you will ever need is one word away. Add a reason clause with から and you have the sentence people actually say: 「あたまが いたいから きょうは いかない」 — my head hurts, so I'm not going today.
      · あたまが いたい。  —  My head hurts.
      · あたまが いたいから きょうは いかない。  —  My head hurts, so I'm not going today.
      · からだが いたいから うちに いる。  —  My body aches, so I'm staying at home.
      · あたまが いたいけど だいじょうぶだ。  —  My head hurts, but I'm all right.
      · あさから あたまが いたい。  —  My head has hurt since this morning.
   NOT: あたまを いたい。 — いたい is an ADJECTIVE — nothing is done TO the head, so it takes が, never を: あたまが いたい。

## Lesson 2 — 「わたしは あたまが いたい」 — は says who, が says which part (ja-m22-neo-2)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「わたしは あたまが いたい」 — は says who, が says which part
   Now put a person in front of it. 「わたしは あたまが いたい」 — as for me, the head is painful. Both particles are doing a job and neither can be dropped: は sets WHO the sentence is about, が picks out WHICH PART of them hurts. English collapses the two into one subject, which is why the shape looks strange the first time and why learners reach for が twice. 「わたしが あたまが いたい」 is the error, and it is a real one — a clause takes one が, and the person is not it. Once you can see the two layers, the same frame carries anything you want to say about somebody else: 「ちちは めが いたい」, 「ミカの おかあさんは みみが いたい」. The possessor rides の on the topic, exactly as it has since module 4, and が stays where it always was, on the part. Drop the topic and the sentence is about you again — that is why 「あたまが いたい」 alone already means MY head.
      · わたしは あたまが いたい。  —  My head hurts.
      · ちちは めが いたいから びょういんに いく。  —  My father's eyes hurt, so he's going to the hospital.
      · あねは みみが いたいけど だいじょうぶだ。  —  My older sister's ear hurts, but she's all right.
      · わたしは げんきだけど おとうとは おなかが いたい。  —  I'm fine, but my younger brother's stomach hurts.
      · ミカの おかあさんは めが いたい。  —  Mika's mother's eyes hurt.
   NOT: わたしが あたまが いたい。 — one clause takes ONE が, and the person is not it — the person is the TOPIC and takes は: わたしは あたまが いたい。

## Lesson 3 — 「ねつが ある」 — a symptom is something that EXISTS (ja-m22-neo-3)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「ねつが ある」 — a symptom is something that EXISTS
   A fever is not something you own in Japanese. It is something that is THERE. 「ねつが ある」 — a fever exists — and that is the ordinary way to say I have a temperature. This is module 6's ある doing exactly what it always did: が marks the thing that exists, and ある is the verb. The English mismatch is the whole difficulty, because 'I have' pushes you towards a を that the sentence has no room for. It generalizes far past symptoms: 「じかんが ない」 I have no time, 「やすみが ある」 I have a day off, 「くすりが ある」 there's medicine. And it sits beside 〜が いたい without clashing, because both use が for the same reason — the thing being talked about is the subject, not the object. 「のどが いたいから ちゃを のむ」, 「ねつが あるから きょうは いかない」: name the symptom, then say what you will do.
      · あさから ねつが ある。  —  I've had a fever since this morning.
      · ねつが あるから きょうは いかない。  —  I have a fever, so I'm not going today.
      · のどが いたいから ちゃを のむ。  —  My throat hurts, so I drink tea.
      · きょうは ねつが ない。  —  I don't have a fever today.
      · じかんが ないから きょうは いかない。  —  I have no time, so I'm not going today.
   NOT: あさから ねつを ある。 — ある says something EXISTS, and what exists takes が, never を: あさから ねつが ある。

## Lesson 5 — 「ときどき あたまが いたい」 — how often (ja-m22-neo-5)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「ときどき あたまが いたい」 — how often
   How often, on one line: いつも always, よく often, ときどき sometimes, あまり not much, ぜんぜん not at all. They sit at the FRONT of the sentence, before everything else, and they change nothing else about it — 「ときどき あたまが いたい」, 「いつも あさに ちゃを のむ」. The last two are the ones to actually learn, because they are not just words, they are a grammar rule: あまり and ぜんぜん can only ever appear with a NEGATIVE predicate. 「あまり くすりを のまない」 is I don't take much medicine. 「あまり くすりを のむ」 is not a slightly odd sentence — it is not a sentence, and a Japanese speaker hears it break. Think of them as half of a pair whose other half is the ない: the adverb opens the negative and the verb closes it. ぜんぜん is the stronger of the two, not at all rather than not much, and it is the one you want when a doctor asks whether something has been happening: 「ぜんぜん たべない」 — I'm not eating at all.
      · あまり くすりを のまない。  —  I don't take much medicine.
      · いつも あさに ちゃを のむ。  —  I always drink tea in the morning.
      · ときどき あたまが いたい。  —  Sometimes my head hurts.
      · よく のどが いたいから いつも ちゃを のむ。  —  My throat often hurts, so I always drink tea.

## Lesson 6 — 「あたまが いたいです」 — telling the doctor (ja-m22-neo-6)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「あたまが いたいです」 — telling the doctor
   This is where politeness stops being decoration. You describe symptoms to a doctor, a pharmacist or a teacher — people you have no claim on — so the whole conversation runs in です・ます, and the plain forms you have been building with all module would land as rude. Two moves cover it. A verb takes ます: ある → あります, のむ → のみます, いく → いきます, and the negative is ません. And いたい, being an adjective, simply adds です with nothing else changing: 「あたまが いたいです」. That です is not the copula finishing a noun — it is the politeness marker sitting on top of an adjective that was already a complete sentence, which is why 「いたいだ」 exists nowhere. The particles do not move an inch: が still marks the part that hurts, を still marks what you take. 「ねつが あります」, 「くすりを のみます」, 「きょうは がっこうに いきません」. Same sentences, different room.
      · ねつが あります。  —  I have a fever.
      · あたまが いたいです。  —  I have a headache.
      · ねつが ありますから きょうは いきません。  —  I have a fever, so I won't be coming today.

      · おなかが いたいですから なにも たべません。  —  My stomach hurts, so I'm not eating anything.
   NOT: ねつが あるです。 — です finishes a NOUN or an adjective — a VERB takes ます instead: ねつが あります。

## Lesson 7 — 「えんぴつを さんぼん」 — counting long thin things (ja-m22-neo-7)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「えんぴつを さんぼん」 — counting long thin things
   〜ほん counts long thin things: pencils, umbrellas, bottles, trees, legs. It goes where every counter goes, after the noun and just before the verb — 「えんぴつを さんぼん かう」. The sound changes are the lesson, and they follow the same shape as every counter you have met. One is ippon. Three is sanBON. Six is roPPON. Ten is juPPON, and eight is haPPON. Everything else keeps the plain ほん: yonhon (4), gohon (5), nanahon (7), kyuuhon (9). To ask, use なんぼん: 「ちゃを なんぼん かう？」 — how many bottles of tea are you buying? The trap is not the sound changes at all. It is that a bare number cannot count a noun in Japanese: 「えんぴつを さん かう」 is not a sentence, and neither is 「えんぴつを さんつ」 — the generic 〜つ counter does not cover a pencil. Pick the counter by the SHAPE of the thing, and 本 is the one for anything longer than it is wide.
      · えんぴつを さんぼん かう。  —  I'll buy three pencils.
      · かさを いっぽん かう。  —  I'll buy an umbrella.
      · ぎゅうにゅうを ろっぽん かうから かねが ない。  —  I'm buying six bottles of milk, so I have no money.
      · ちゃを なんぼん かう？  —  How many bottles of tea are you buying?
      · やまに きが いっぽん ある。  —  There's one tree on the mountain.
   NOT: えんぴつを さん かう。 — a bare number cannot count anything in Japanese — a long thin thing takes 〜ほん, and after さん it turns into ぼん: えんぴつを さんぼん かう。

## Lesson 9 — 「くすりを ください」 — at the pharmacy (ja-m22-neo-9)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「くすりを ください」 — at the pharmacy
   A pharmacy is the shortest useful conversation in the language. Name what you want with を and finish with ください: 「くすりを ください」 — medicine, please. ください IS the verb of the sentence, and it is polite on its own, which is why it works with a stranger behind a counter with nothing else added. Put すみません in front to get their attention first, and you have the whole transaction. The count goes in its usual slot, straight before ください: 「みずを ごほん ください」, five bottles of water. Drop the number and the sentence is still perfectly good — 「みずを ください」 — so the counter buys you precision, not grammar. And you can say WHY, which is what actually gets you the right thing: 「あたまが いたいから くすりを ください」. The one error worth naming is が. What you are asking for is what you want handed over, so it takes を; が would make the medicine the thing doing something.
      · すみません くすりを ください。  —  Excuse me — some medicine, please.
      · ちゃを よんほん ください。  —  Four bottles of tea, please.
      · みずを ごほん ください。  —  Five bottles of water, please.
      · あたまが いたいから くすりを ください。  —  My head hurts, so could I have some medicine.
      · くすりと みずを ください。  —  Some medicine and some water, please.
   NOT: すみません くすりが ください。 — what you are ASKING FOR takes を — が would make the medicine the thing doing something: すみません くすりを ください。

## Lesson 10 — 「のまないで ください」 — what the doctor tells you not to do (ja-m22-neo-10)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「のまないで ください」 — what the doctor tells you not to do
   The other half of a pharmacy visit is what you are told NOT to do, and it is built from something you already own. Take the ない-form, add で, finish with ください: のむ → のまない → 「のまないで ください」, please don't drink it. Nothing else changes and no verb is irregular here — たべない → たべないで, いかない → いかないで, みない → みないで. The で is what turns a flat negative into a request; without it the sentence stops halfway and means nothing. It is a REQUEST rather than a law, which is exactly the register a doctor uses on a patient: 「おさけを のまないで ください」 is please don't drink alcohol, where a sign on a wall would say のんでは いけません instead. And because ください is polite by itself, you can hear this from a stranger and say it back to one — it is the same word you used to ask for the medicine, doing the opposite job.
      · おさけを のまないで ください。  —  Please don't drink alcohol.

## Lesson 11 — 「たべても いい？」 — asking whether you may (ja-m22-neo-11)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「たべても いい？」 — asking whether you may
   And the question you actually want to ask a doctor is the opposite one: may I? Put も after the て-form and finish with いい — 「くすりを のんでも いい？」, is it all right if I take the medicine? Literally: even if I drink it, it's fine. Said flat instead of asked, the same words GRANT the permission: 「のんでも いい」 you may take it. The answers are short — うん、いい for go ahead, ううん、だめ for no. Two things make this lesson worth having beside ないでください. First, they are one contrast, not two facts: 「たべても いい？」 and 「たべないで ください」 are the two ends of the same conversation, and a patient asks the first while a doctor says the second. Second, the polite version costs one word: 「くすりを のんでも いいですか」. Same frame, です on the いい, か on the end — and that is the form you will use with anyone in a white coat.
      · くすりを のんでも いい？  —  Is it OK if I take the medicine?
      · すしを たべても いい？  —  May I eat sushi?
      · きょうは がっこうに いっても いい？  —  May I go to school today?
      · うみで およいでも いい？  —  May I swim in the sea?
      · けいたいを みても いいですか。  —  May I look at my phone?
   NOT: くすりを のむも いい？ — も and いい hang off the て-form — のむ becomes のんで: くすりを のんでも いい？
