# m29 — Register mastery + N5 capstone

Module 29 · JLPT N5 complete

The last module of N5, and the one where you stop learning new things and start choosing. 「じゃない」「じゃないです」「じゃありません」 are one meaning in three registers; 「よ」 hands somebody news and 「ね」 asks them to nod. Then the same switch across everything you own — the negative, the past, the explanation 「〜んですよ」, the invitation 「いきませんか」, and the refusal that never says no: 「ばんは ちょっと。」 Every sentence is new.

## Lesson 1 — 「わたしのじゃない」 — it isn't, said three ways (ja-m29-neo-1)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「わたしのじゃない」 — it isn't, said three ways
   You have been saying 「じゃない」 since module 12 — it is how a noun sentence goes negative. 「わたしのじゃない」, it isn't mine. This lesson adds no new meaning at all. It adds the two other ways to say the same thing, because Japanese does not pick one way of saying something and use it everywhere: it picks the one that fits the person listening. To a friend it stays 「じゃない」. To anybody you are already using です with — a teacher, somebody you met an hour ago, the person at the next desk — it becomes 「じゃないです」. And when you want to sound careful, at a counter or on the phone to a company or in anything written down, it becomes 「じゃありません」. Three forms, one meaning, and the choice is not about the sentence. Now look at what actually changes: only the END. The noun in front never moves, the は never moves, and there is no polite version of the noun itself. Politeness in Japanese is carried once and it is carried at the end, which is exactly what です and ます taught you in module 7. One warning, because everyone walks into it: this whole family is for NOUNS and な-adjectives. An い-adjective goes negative on its own — 「たかくない」 — and 「たかいじゃない」 is not a sentence.
      · この かぎは わたしのじゃない。  —  This key isn't mine.
      · きゅうりは くだものじゃないです。  —  A cucumber isn't a fruit.
      · さかなは にくじゃありません。  —  Fish isn't meat.
      · ごごは ひまじゃない。  —  I'm not free in the afternoon.
      · たなかさんは いしゃじゃありません。せんせいです。  —  Tanaka isn't a doctor. He's a teacher.
   NOT: この かぎは わたしのじゃないだ。 — じゃない IS the finished negative — nothing follows it, and だ never sits on top of it: この かぎは わたしのじゃない。

## Lesson 2 — 「ゆきが ふるよ」 — telling somebody something new (ja-m29-neo-2)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「ゆきが ふるよ」 — telling somebody something new
   よ goes on the very end of a finished sentence and it means one thing: this is news to you. You have something the listener does not have, and よ is how you hand it over. 「ゆきが ふるよ。」 — it's going to snow, and I can hear that you don't know that yet. Say the same sentence without よ and it is a flat remark about the weather; say it with よ and it is aimed at somebody. That is the whole job, and it is worth having, because a beginner's Japanese without よ sounds oddly like a weather report even when it is a warning. Two mechanical facts, and neither is negotiable. First, よ attaches to a sentence that is ALREADY finished, in whatever register you finished it: 「ふるよ。」 to a friend, 「いきますよ。」 to a teacher, 「じゃないですよ。」 when you are correcting somebody politely. The politeness is decided before よ arrives, and よ neither adds any nor takes any away. Second, nothing follows よ. It is the last thing in the sentence. There is one thing NOT to do with it. Do not put よ on something you can both plainly see. Telling somebody standing in the same snow 「さむいよ」 informs them of their own experience, which lands somewhere between odd and rude — that sentence wants ね, and ね is the next lesson.
      · ゆきが ふるよ。  —  It's going to snow, you know.
      · この おんがくは いいよ。  —  This music is good, you know.
      · かぎは つくえの うえに あるよ。  —  The key's on the desk, you know.
      · せんせい じかんが ありませんよ。  —  Say politely: Teacher, there isn't any time
      · へやは せまいよ。  —  The room's narrow, you know.
   NOT: ゆきよが ふる。 — よ goes on the very END of a finished sentence and never inside it — it is not a particle marking a word: ゆきが ふるよ。

## Lesson 3 — 「かぜが つよいね」 — checking that you agree (ja-m29-neo-3)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「かぜが つよいね」 — checking that you agree
   ね is the exact opposite move to よ, and the pair is easier to learn together than apart. よ says I know something you don't. ね says we both know this, don't we — and it asks the listener to nod. 「かぜが つよいね。」 — the wind's strong, isn't it. You are not informing anybody; you are checking that you are standing in the same weather. That is why ね is everywhere in real conversation and why a beginner who only has よ sounds strangely combative. Mechanically it behaves exactly like よ. It sits at the very end, it attaches to a sentence that is already finished, and it takes whatever register that sentence was in: 「きれいだね。」 to a friend, 「きれいですね。」 to a teacher. Nothing follows it. And the two are a genuine choice, not a decoration: telling somebody the key is on the desk takes よ because they were looking for it, while remarking that the room is cold takes ね because they are in it. Pick the wrong one and the sentence is still grammatical — it is just aimed at the wrong person. One extra use worth knowing now. ね also softens a sentence that might otherwise land too hard, which is why 「ちょっと まってね。」 is friendlier than 「ちょっと まって。」
      · この はなは きれいだね。  —  These flowers are pretty, aren't they.
      · かぜが つよいね。  —  The wind's strong, isn't it.
      · たなかさんは えいごが じょうずですね。  —  Say politely: Tanaka, you're good at English, aren't you
      · あきは すずしいね。  —  Autumn is cool, isn't it.
      · その くつは ちょっと ちいさいね。  —  Those shoes are a bit small, aren't they.
   NOT: この はなは きれいねだ。 — ね goes AFTER the finished sentence, and だ is what finishes it — so だ comes first and ね last: この はなは きれいだね。

## Lesson 5 — ともだちと せんせい — the same thing, two ways to say it (ja-m29-neo-5)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — ともだちと せんせい — the same thing, two ways to say it
   You now own two complete systems for saying the same thing, and this lesson is about moving between them quickly. To a friend a verb stays in its dictionary form and a noun sentence ends in だ. To a teacher the verb takes ます and the noun sentence ends in です. What is worth noticing is how little else changes: same topic, same particles, same word order, same meaning — only the last word moves. That is the shape of the whole language, politeness bolted onto the end rather than a different way of building the sentence. Two things follow. The register is chosen by the AUDIENCE, not by the topic — there is no polite subject matter and no casual one. And the switch happens at SPEED, because the person you are talking to can change mid-room. That is the skill: not knowing both forms, which you already do, but reaching for the right one without stopping to think.
      · なつやすみに がいこくに いくよ。  —  Say to a friend: I'm going abroad in the summer holidays
      · なつやすみに がいこくに いきますよ。  —  Say politely: I'll be going abroad in the summer holidays
      · へやが せまいから つくえを かいます。  —  Say politely: I'm going to buy a desk, because the room is narrow
      · ばんは とりにくを たべるよ。  —  Say to a friend: I eat chicken in the evening
      · たなかさんに えいごを ききますね。  —  Say politely: I'll ask Tanaka about English, then

## Lesson 6 — 「いかない」と「いきません」 — saying no to two different people (ja-m29-neo-6)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「いかない」と「いきません」 — saying no to two different people
   Saying no is where register shows up first, because a refusal is the one thing you cannot mumble past. The plain negative is the ない-form you have had since module 6, and the polite negative is ません on the ます-stem: 「いかない」 and 「いきません」, 「たべない」 and 「たべません」, 「しらない」 and 「しりません」. Same meaning, same slot at the end of the sentence, different listener. Two things are worth pinning down. First, a verb goes negative ONCE. There is no 「いかないません」 — you pick the plain ending or the polite one and that is the whole decision. Second, this is the same layering you have just seen with じゃない: politeness lives at the END, so the reason clause in front of a negative stays plain even when the negative is polite. 「じかんが ないから いきません。」 is a perfectly ordinary polite sentence with a plain clause inside it, and trying to make the から half polite as well is the commonest way to make a sentence sound like a textbook. And the negatives take よ and ね exactly as the positives do: 「しらないよ」 when you are telling somebody, 「しりませんね」 when you are agreeing that neither of you does.

## Lesson 7 — 「いった」と「いきました」 — telling somebody what happened (ja-m29-neo-7)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「いった」と「いきました」 — telling somebody what happened
   Telling somebody what happened is the other half of the switch, and it works the way you would now expect. The plain past is the た-form from module 11 and the polite past is ました on the ます-stem: 「いった」 and 「いきました」, 「たべた」 and 「たべました」, 「のんだ」 and 「のみました」. The copula moves in step — 「だった」 to a friend, 「でした」 to a teacher — so 「はれだった」 and 「はれでした」 are the same afternoon reported to two different people. The negative past is where the two systems look least alike and it is worth learning as a pair rather than deriving each time: plain 「なかった」 against polite 「ませんでした」. 「じかんが なかった。」 and 「じかんが ありませんでした。」 both say there was no time. Notice that the polite negative past is the longest thing in this module and that it is ONE unit — ません plus でした, with nothing between them. Two habits to build now. A past-tense sentence almost always arrives with its reason attached, and から takes the plain form in front of it whatever register the sentence ends in. And ね belongs on a shared memory — 「たのしかったね」 is what you say about something you both did.
      · ごぜんは はれだったから さんぽに いった。  —  Say to a friend: It was fine this morning, so I went for a walk

      · じかんが なかったから でんわを しませんでした。  —  Say politely: I didn't phone, because I hadn't got time
      · その おんがくは よかったよ。  —  Say to a friend: That music was good

## Lesson 9 — 「〜んですよ」と「〜んですね」 — explaining, and hearing an explanation (ja-m29-neo-9)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「〜んですよ」と「〜んですね」 — explaining, and hearing an explanation
   んだ and んです were module 27's item, and this is the lesson where they become a register pair like everything else. The job has not changed: ん turns a plain sentence into an explanation of the situation you are both in. What changes is the last syllable — 「ないんだ」 to a friend, 「ないんです」 to anybody else — and, once again, everything in FRONT of ん stays plain. 「かぜが つよいんです。」 has a plain adjective inside a polite sentence, and that is not a mistake; it is the rule. Two details worth having explicitly. After a NOUN or a な-adjective, ん needs な in front of it: 「やすみなんだ」, never 「やすみんだ」. And ん takes よ and ね on the outside exactly as a plain sentence does, which produces the two most useful conversational endings in the language: 「〜んですよ」, the thing I am explaining is news to you, and 「〜んですね」, so THAT is what is going on — the sentence you say when somebody's explanation has just made sense of something. Use them. A learner who can say 「ああ、そうなんですね。」 at the right moment sounds several months further along than one who cannot.

## Lesson 10 — 「いかない？」と「いきませんか」 — asking somebody along (ja-m29-neo-10)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「いかない？」と「いきませんか」 — asking somebody along
   An invitation in Japanese is a negative question, which sounds backwards until you notice English does the same thing: won't you come along? Casually you ask 「いかない？」 with a rise at the end, and politely you ask 「いきませんか。」 with か on it. Both mean shall we — the negative is doing politeness work rather than negative work, because asking whether somebody is NOT doing a thing leaves them an easy way to say no. That is the whole reason the form exists, and it is why 「いく？」 is a question about your plans while 「いかない？」 is an invitation. The register split is the usual one and the same warning applies as everywhere else: か belongs to the POLITE question. A casual question is marked by intonation and nothing else, so 「いかないか？」 is not the friendly version of anything. Once the other person has agreed, the sentence you want is ましょう — 「いきましょう。」, let's go — which is the polite let's, and which you have had since module 24. And a refusal is coming in two lessons: the polite way to turn an invitation down is not a negative sentence at all.

## Lesson 11 — 「ばんは ちょっと。」 — saying no without saying it (ja-m29-neo-11)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「ばんは ちょっと。」 — saying no without saying it
   This is the last thing N5 owes you and it may be the most used word in the language. ちょっと literally means a little, and it does mean that — 「ちょっと たかい」, a bit expensive. But its real job is to take the edge off a sentence that would otherwise be too direct, and Japanese is a language where a bare no is genuinely uncomfortable. So an invitation is refused like this: 「ばんは ちょっと。」 The sentence stops. Nothing follows ちょっと, and the person you are talking to understands completely, because trailing off IS the refusal. Saying 「いきません」 to a friendly invitation is grammatically perfect and socially wrong, and this is the fix. Three ways to use it, in order of how often you will need them. As a refusal: ちょっと on its own, or with a reason politely attached — 「すみません ばんは ちょっと じかんが ないんです。」 As a softener on a criticism: 「その ぼうしは ちょっと おおきいですね。」 is what a Japanese speaker says instead of the hat is too big. And literally, meaning a small amount, which is where it started. The ね from lesson 3 does the same softening work, which is why the two so often turn up in the same sentence.
      · ちょっと たかいから かわない。  —  Say to a friend: It's a bit expensive, so I won't buy it
      · ばんは ちょっと。  —  Say to a friend: This evening is a bit… (a refusal)
      · すみません ごぜんは ちょっと じかんが ないんです。  —  Say politely: Sorry, I haven't quite got time this morning
      · その ぼうしは ちょっと おおきいですね。  —  Say politely: That hat is a bit big, isn't it
      · ちょっと まってね。  —  Say to a friend: Hang on a second
   NOT: ちょっと たかいだから かわない。 — an い-adjective finishes its clause on its own and never takes だ — から attaches straight onto it: ちょっと たかいから かわない。
