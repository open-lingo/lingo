# m25 — Conjecture

Module 25 · Weather

「あしたは あめでしょう」 — it'll probably rain tomorrow. でしょう is not a verb ending; it is a word that stands behind a finished sentence and turns a claim into an expectation. A noun drops its だ, an adjective changes nothing, a verb changes nothing. Then the weather it was made for, the four seasons, and the two adverbs that agree with it — たぶん and きっと. Finally the register neighbours: 「あついでしょ？」 asking a friend to agree, 「あした あめかな」 wondering out loud, and だろう, which you will hear everywhere and say only to yourself.

## Lesson 1 — 「あしたは あめでしょう」 — the ending a forecast is made of (ja-m25-neo-1)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「あしたは あめでしょう」 — the ending a forecast is made of
   でしょう means probably, and it is not a verb ending — it is a word that stands BEHIND a finished sentence and changes what you are claiming with it. 「あしたは ゆきだ」 says tomorrow is snow, flatly, as a fact you are vouching for. 「あしたは ゆきでしょう。」 says you expect snow: it is how it looks from here, and you are not staking anything on it. That is the whole meaning. It is not the future — 「ゆきだ」 is already about tomorrow — and it is not obligation. Probably, I expect, I imagine. There is one mechanical thing to get right and it is the だ. A noun sentence needs だ to be a sentence at all, but でしょう does the finishing itself, so the だ goes: 「ゆきだ」 becomes 「ゆきでしょう」, never 「ゆきだでしょう」. Same for な-adjectives, which behave like nouns everywhere: 「しずかだ」 becomes 「しずかでしょう」. One more thing worth knowing before you use it. でしょう is the NEUTRAL form — it is what the weather forecast says, what a shop assistant says, what your friend says — so unlike ます it is not a politeness decision you have to make. Say it to anybody.
      · あしたは ゆきでしょう。  —  It'll probably snow tomorrow.
      · あの ひとは せんせいでしょう。  —  That person over there is probably a teacher.
      · きょうは やすみでしょう。  —  Today's probably a day off.
      · これは ケンの かさでしょう。  —  This is probably Ken's umbrella.
      · ミカは いま としょかんでしょう。  —  Mika's probably at the library right now.
   NOT: あしたは ゆきだでしょう。 — でしょう finishes the sentence itself, so a noun drops its だ in front of it: あしたは ゆきでしょう。

## Lesson 2 — 「さむいでしょう」 — adjectives take it bare (ja-m25-neo-2)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「さむいでしょう」 — adjectives take it bare
   An い-adjective is already a whole sentence on its own — that has been true since module 12 — so でしょう attaches to it with nothing removed and nothing added. 「さむい」 becomes 「さむいでしょう」. 「おいしい」 becomes 「おいしいでしょう」. There is no い to drop, no だ to delete, no form to look up: you say the adjective and then you say でしょう. This is the easiest rung of the three and it is the one you will use most, because most of what anybody guesses about is what something is LIKE. な-adjectives go the other way and they go exactly the way nouns did in the last lesson: they need だ to be a sentence, でしょう does the finishing instead, so the だ disappears. 「しずかだ」 becomes 「しずかでしょう」, 「きれいだ」 becomes 「きれいでしょう」. So the question to ask yourself is the same one module 12 taught you: is this an い-adjective or a な-adjective? い-adjective, add でしょう. な-adjective, swap だ for でしょう. Nothing in this rule is new — only where you are pointing it.
      · あしたは さむいでしょう。  —  It'll probably be cold tomorrow.
      · やまは とおいでしょう。  —  The mountain's probably a long way off.
      · その えいがは おもしろいでしょう。  —  That film's probably interesting.
      · としょかんは しずかでしょう。  —  The library's probably quiet.
      · きょうは あたたかいでしょう。  —  It's probably mild today.
   NOT: あしたは さむいだでしょう。 — an い-adjective is already a complete sentence and never takes だ — in front of でしょう or anywhere else: あしたは さむいでしょう。

## Lesson 3 — 「あめが ふるでしょう」 — verbs take it bare too (ja-m25-neo-3)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「あめが ふるでしょう」 — verbs take it bare too
   The third rung is the one that looks hardest and is actually free: a plain verb takes でしょう exactly as it stands. 「ふる」 becomes 「ふるでしょう」. 「くる」 becomes 「くるでしょう」. Nothing is dropped, because a dictionary-form verb has been a complete sentence since module 5 and it never wanted a だ in the first place. The past works the same way — 「たべた」 becomes 「たべたでしょう」, they've probably eaten — and so does the negative: 「こない」 becomes 「こないでしょう」, they probably won't come. One verb is worth meeting here on its own account. ふる is what Japanese uses for anything that comes down out of the sky, and it takes the thing that falls as its subject with が: 「あめが ふる」, it rains; 「ゆきが ふる」, it snows. English needs two different verbs for those and Japanese needs one, which is why a forecast can say 「ゆきが ふるでしょう」 without ever mentioning weather. And くもり — cloud cover — is a NOUN, so it goes back to the first rung: 「あしたは くもりでしょう。」
      · あした あめが ふるでしょう。  —  It'll probably rain tomorrow.
      · ミカは あした じてんしゃで くるでしょう。  —  Mika will probably come by bicycle tomorrow.
      · トムは もう すしを たべたでしょう。  —  Tom's probably eaten the sushi already.
      · ケンは いそがしいから こないでしょう。  —  Ken's busy, so he probably won't come.
      · あさは くもりでしょうけど ばんは ゆきが ふるでしょう。  —  It'll probably be cloudy in the morning, but it'll probably snow in the evening.
   NOT: あした あめが ふるだでしょう。 — a verb is already a whole sentence and never takes だ — でしょう attaches to the dictionary form as it stands: あした あめが ふるでしょう。

## Lesson 5 — 「ふゆは さむいでしょう」 — four seasons, two particles (ja-m25-neo-5)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「ふゆは さむいでしょう」 — four seasons, two particles
   The four seasons — はる spring, なつ summer, あき autumn, ふゆ winter — are ordinary nouns, and the particle you put on one decides what kind of sentence you are making. は TOPICS the season — you are about to say what it is LIKE: 「ふゆは さむい」, winter is cold. に says WHEN something happened or will happen: 「はるに さくらを みる」, I look at the cherry blossom in spring. That に is module 11's に and the rule has not changed a word: it goes on times you could point to on a calendar or a clock, and a season is exactly that kind of time. The trap is putting に on the first kind of sentence. 「ふゆに さむい」 is wrong, because nothing HAPPENS in it — cold is not an event, it is what winter is like, and そこ needs は. Test yourself with one question: is there a verb? If there is an event, に is available. If you are only describing the season, use は. Two small notes. なつ and なつやすみ are different words — one is the season, one is the holiday inside it. And the seasons pair naturally with everything you learned last module about comparing: 「なつは はるより あつい」.
      · ふゆは さむいでしょう。  —  Winter's probably cold.
      · はるに やまで さくらを みる。  —  I look at the cherry blossom on the mountain in spring.
      · はるは あたたかいでしょう。  —  Spring's probably mild.
      · ふゆに やまで ゆきが ふるでしょう。  —  It'll probably snow on the mountain in winter.
      · はるは いい てんきでしょう。  —  The weather's probably nice in spring.
   NOT: ふゆに さむいでしょう。 — に marks WHEN something happens, and being cold is not an event — a season you are simply describing takes は: ふゆは さむいでしょう。

## Lesson 6 — 「たぶん あめでしょう」 — how sure, said at the front (ja-m25-neo-6)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「たぶん あめでしょう」 — how sure, said at the front
   Japanese likes to tell you how sure the speaker is before the sentence has finished, and it does it with an adverb at the front. たぶん is probably — it agrees with でしょう and the two travel together so often that 「たぶん あめでしょう」 sounds more natural than either half alone. きっと is the strong one: surely, definitely, I'm certain. 「きっと はれでしょう。」 The pair is worth learning together because they mark the two ends of the same scale and English blurs them — "probably" and "surely" both come out as "I think" half the time. Both are ADVERBS, so they take no particle at all and they sit at the FRONT, before the topic if there is one: 「たぶん あしたは あめでしょう」 or 「あしたは たぶん あめでしょう」, both fine, but never wedged in between the noun and でしょう. And they do not replace でしょう — the adverb says how sure you are, the ending says you are guessing, and a forecast wants both. One habit worth picking up now: たぶん is the single most common hedge in spoken Japanese, so if you are unsure whether to commit to something, put たぶん in front of it and you have said so.

## Lesson 7 — 「あした あめかな」 — wondering, out loud (ja-m25-neo-7)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「あした あめかな」 — wondering, out loud
   かな is what you say when you are wondering at yourself. 「あした あめかな。」 — I wonder if it'll rain tomorrow. It is built out of か, the question particle, and it keeps a little of か's shape, but it is NOT a question: nobody has been asked anything. You are turning the thought over out loud, and if the person beside you answers, that is them joining in rather than you addressing them. That is why the English is always I wonder — never Is it? Attachment is the same rule you have used all module. A noun or な-adjective drops its だ: 「あめだ」 → 「あめかな」. An い-adjective takes it as it stands: 「さむいかな」. A verb takes it as it stands: 「くるかな」. Where かな really earns its place is with a question word, because that is a wondering English has no short way to say: 「あの ひとは だれかな」 — I wonder who that is. 「じゅぎょうは なんじかな」 — I wonder what time the class is. It is casual, so keep it for friends and for yourself, and be careful of one thing: 「〜かな」 aimed straight at somebody can land as a hint that you want them to do something, which is a subtlety for later. Wonder out loud and you will always be safe.

## Lesson 9 — 「なんだろう」 — the one you mostly just hear (ja-m25-neo-9)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「なんだろう」 — the one you mostly just hear
   だろう is でしょう with the polite layer taken off, and this is the one place in the course where the plain form is NOT the one you produce. The reason is social rather than grammatical. Aimed at a listener, sentence-final だろう lands blunt and markedly masculine — 「あした あめだろう」 said to somebody has a flavour English can only spell as "it'll rain, obviously" — so a learner who reaches for it as the casual twin of でしょう will sound rude in rooms where they meant to sound relaxed. でしょう is neutral and でしょ is the casual one. Say those. You still need to UNDERSTAND だろう, because you will hear it constantly: in books, in songs, from men speaking casually, and in narration. So recognise it and let it mean exactly what でしょう means. There is ONE place you should use it, and you will use it every day. After a question word there is no listener at all — you are talking to yourself — and だろう is the natural ending for that: 「なんだろう。」 what IS that? 「だれだろう。」 who's that, I wonder? 「なんじだろう。」 what time is it, I wonder? Nobody has been addressed, so nothing can land blunt. Understand it everywhere, say it only to yourself.

## Lesson 10 — 「あついでしょ？」 — handing the thought over (ja-m25-neo-10)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「あついでしょ？」 — handing the thought over
   でしょ is でしょう with the tail clipped, and the shortening does what shortening always does in Japanese — it makes the sentence casual. 「あした あめでしょ。」 is 「あした あめでしょう。」 said to a friend. Attachment does not change: だ still disappears after a noun, an い-adjective still takes it bare. But でしょ has a second job that でしょう barely does, and it is the reason you will hear it forty times a day. Say it with a RISING contour and it stops being a guess and becomes an appeal: 「あついでしょ？」 — it's hot, right? You are not informing the listener and you are not really asking either; you are handing them a thought you are confident they share and inviting them to agree. English does this with a tag question — isn't it, right?, don't you think? — and Japanese does it with でしょ？. Two things follow. First, it only works when you genuinely expect agreement, so it is a warm move between friends and a pushy one with a stranger. Second, keep it out of polite conversation entirely: with a teacher or in a shop, the neutral でしょう is what you want.
      · あついでしょ？  —  It's hot, right?
      · その えいがは おもしろいでしょ？  —  That film's interesting, right?
      · あしたは やすみでしょ？  —  Tomorrow's a day off, right?
      · ミカは くるでしょ？  —  Mika's coming, right?
      · あした あめでしょ。  —  It'll probably rain tomorrow. — falling, just casual でしょう
   NOT: あついだでしょ？ — an い-adjective never takes だ, and でしょ attaches to it bare exactly as でしょう does: あついでしょ？

## Lesson 11 — 「ふると おもう」 と 「ふるでしょう」 — whose view, or how likely (ja-m25-neo-11)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「ふると おもう」 と 「ふるでしょう」 — whose view, or how likely
   You have had two ways to hedge since module 18 and it is time to say what the difference is, because English hides it. 〜と おもう says WHOSE opinion this is: yours. でしょう says HOW SURE the claim is, and it leaves the judgement open for the listener to share. 「あした あめが ふると おもう」 is my read on it. 「あした あめでしょう」 is how it looks — which is why the weather forecast says でしょう every single evening and never once says とおもう: a forecaster is not offering a personal opinion, they are reporting a likelihood. Use とおもう when the point is that it is YOUR view — 「その えいがは おもしろいと おもう」 — and でしょう when the point is that the thing is likely and anybody looking would say the same. There is a mechanical difference too, and it is a clean one to check yourself against: とおもう takes a complete plain sentence, so a noun KEEPS its だ — 「あめだと おもう」 — while でしょう does the finishing itself, so the noun LOSES it: 「あめでしょう」. Same noun, opposite treatment, because one is a quotation and the other is an ending.
      · あした あめが ふると おもう。  —  I think it'll rain tomorrow.
      · あした あめでしょう。  —  It'll probably rain tomorrow.
      · なつは あついと おもう。  —  I think summer's hot.
      · ふゆは さむいでしょう。  —  Winter's probably cold.
      · あしたは はれだと おもう。  —  I think it'll be clear tomorrow.
   NOT: あした あめが ふるでしょうと おもう。 — でしょう is built on です and politeness never goes inside a quotation — module 18's rule, the one that made たべますと おもう wrong: あした あめが ふると おもう。
