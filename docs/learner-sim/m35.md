# m35 — Give & receive II — てあげる/てくれる/てもらう + favors

Module 35 · JLPT N4

No new rule this time — module 30's て + helper slot, with module 31's giving verbs parked in it, drilled until it's reflex. せんせいが おしえてくれた reports a kindness that landed on you; ともだちに てつだってもらった tells the same help from your own end. The trap worth knowing: offering upward with あげる — せんせいを てつだってあげます — is grammatically perfect and socially wrong, so this module teaches the mistake on purpose before handing you the real move, てつだいましょうか. Then the whole favor-asking ladder, casual て/てくれる？/てくれない？ up through polite てくれませんか/てもらえますか, assembled for real for the first time.

## Lesson 1 — 「おしえてくれた」 — help that lands on you (ja-m35-neo-1)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「おしえてくれた」 — help that lands on you
   Module 30 gave you the slot: a て-form with an ordinary verb parked behind it, the pair acting as one predicate. Module 31 gave you くれる: giving that arrives at my side. Put them together — 「せんせいが おしえてくれた」 — and the sentence does two jobs at once: the teacher taught me, AND the verb itself files it as a kindness that landed on me. There is no new rule in this module. が still marks the giver, the て-verb still never moves, くれる still points inward and carries all the tense. English has no slot for this, which is why English speakers under-use it — but in Japanese, leaving くれる off a sentence like this doesn't sound neutral, it sounds like the help didn't count.
      · せんせいが おしえてくれた。  —  The teacher taught me (and I'm glad of it).
      · ともだちが てつだってくれる。  —  My friend helps me out.
      · あねが ばんごはんを かってくれた。  —  My older sister bought me dinner.
   NOT: せんせいが おしえてあげた。 — あげる points AWAY from the speaker — this says the teacher taught somebody else, as a favor from the teacher's side. Help that reached YOU takes くれる: おしえてくれた.

## Lesson 2 — 「かってあげた」 — and the trap in pointing it upward (ja-m35-neo-2)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「かってあげた」 — and the trap in pointing it upward
   〜てあげる is the outward half: I do something FOR somebody. 「ともだちに ケーキを かってあげた」 — I bought my friend a cake, and the verb frames it as a favor from me. Use it freely about friends, family, children. But hear the frame: あげる bestows. Aimed UPWARD — at a teacher, a boss, a customer — 「てつだってあげます」 says "I shall do you the favor of helping," and it lands as condescension, however kind you meant it. This is not a grammar error; the sentence is perfectly formed. It is a register error, which is worse, because nobody corrects it. What you say instead is coming in lesson 10: offer with ましょうか and keep あげる out of it.
      · ともだちに ケーキを かってあげた。  —  I bought my friend a cake.
      · いもうとに えいごを おしえてあげる。  —  I'll teach my little sister English.
      · ケンに じしょを かしてあげた。  —  I lent Ken my dictionary.
   NOT: せんせいに ケーキを かってあげた。 — grammatically flawless, socially wrong: あげる frames the gift as a favor bestowed downward, and pointed at your teacher it condescends. Give upward without the verb of bestowing — or offer with ましょうか (lesson 10).

## Lesson 3 — 「てつだってもらった」 — the same help, told from your end (ja-m35-neo-3)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「てつだってもらった」 — the same help, told from your end
   〜てもらう is the same event as 〜てくれる, told from the other end — module 31's mirror, now with actions in it. 「ともだちが てつだってくれた」: my friend helped me, friend as subject. 「ともだちに てつだってもらった」: I got my friend's help, ME as subject, friend marked with に. English has no verb for this flip, so read it as "I got (someone) to…" or "I had (someone)…". Why bother, when くれる already says it? Because もらう makes the RECEIVING the story — I needed help and I went and got it. くれる reports their kindness; もらう reports my solution.
      · ともだちに てつだってもらった。  —  I got my friend to help me.
      · せんせいに おしえてもらう。  —  I get the teacher to teach me.
      · あにに かばんを もってもらった。  —  I had my brother carry my bag.
   NOT: ともだちが てつだってもらった。 — もらう makes the RECEIVER the subject — with ともだち marked が, this says your friend received the help, from someone unnamed. For "my friend helped me," either flip the particle (ともだちに…もらった) or flip the verb (ともだちが…くれた).

## Lesson 5 — 「はこんでくれない？」 — asking, the friendly way (ja-m35-neo-5)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「はこんでくれない？」 — asking, the friendly way
   Now the asking. You have requested with 〜てください since module 8 — polite, safe, and a little stiff between friends. The casual rungs are built from くれる: 「てつだってくれる？」 asks "will you help me?" straight; 「てつだってくれない？」 asks it through the negative — "won't you help me?" — and the negative SOFTENS it, exactly the way ませんか softened invitations back in module 24. Softer through the negative: it is the same move, one register down. Between friends, 〜てくれない？ is the everyday ask. Below both sits the bare て-form — 「てつだって」 — family-close, or urgent.
      · ちょっと てつだってくれる？  —  Give me a hand?
      · てつだってくれない？  —  Won't you help me out?
      · まって！  —  Wait!
   NOT: せんせい、てつだってくれない？ — the くれない？ rung is friend-register — aimed at your teacher it is over-familiar. The polite rungs exist for exactly this: てつだってくれませんか.

## Lesson 6 — 「とってもらえますか」 — asking, the polite way (ja-m35-neo-6)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「とってもらえますか」 — asking, the polite way
   The polite rungs, and the top of the ladder. 「てつだってくれませんか」 is くれない？ in polite dress — same negative softening, ます layer on. 「てつだってもらえますか」 climbs one higher by switching verbs: it runs on もらう in the POTENTIAL (module 24's form) — literally "would I be able to receive your helping?" — which asks about possibility instead of willingness, and that indirection is the politeness. Against them both sits module 8's てください, which for all its です-class dress is a DIRECTIVE — fine for a shop counter, blunt for a favor. Rule of thumb: ください tells, くれませんか asks, もらえますか asks whether asking is even possible.
      · すみません、てつだってくれませんか。  —  Excuse me, could you help me?
      · しゃしんを とってもらえますか。  —  Could I get you to take a photo?
      · ちょっと まってください。  —  Please wait a moment.
   NOT: すみません、てつだってもらいませんか。 — the top rung runs on the POTENTIAL — もらえますか asks whether receiving your help is possible. もらいませんか drops the potential and literally invites the listener to receive something from you, the reverse of the favor you meant.

## Lesson 7 — 「ケンだけ」と「ひとりしか いない」 — just, and only (ja-m35-neo-7)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「ケンだけ」と「ひとりしか いない」 — just, and only
   だけ is "just this, no more," and it sits directly after the thing it limits: 「ひとつだけ」 just one, 「ケンだけ」 only Ken. The machinery around it stays positive — 「ケンだけが てつだってくれた」, only Ken helped me. It stacks onto the noun BEFORE the particle where there is one: だけが, だけに. That is the whole word. Its dark twin — the one that needs a negative — is next.
      · ケンだけが てつだってくれた。  —  Only Ken helped me.
      · ひとつだけ ください。  —  Just one, please.
      · みずだけ のむ。  —  I'll just drink water.
   NOT: ケンだけが てつだってくれない。 — だけ + a negative says only Ken DOESN'T help — almost the opposite of "only Ken helps." For only-Ken-and-nobody-else, keep だけ positive, or reach for しか…ない, which is built for the missing-everyone-else reading.
2. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「ケンだけ」と「ひとりしか いない」 — just, and only
   しか is だけ's shadow: it means "only" too, but it REQUIRES the negative — しか…ない — and it counts what is missing rather than what is there. 「ひとりしか いない」: there is only one person, said as "nobody beyond one." The feeling is scarcity: 「せんえんしか ない」 laments the mere thousand yen; 「せんえんだけ ある」 just states it. One mechanical difference to drill: しか REPLACES が and を outright (ケンしか こない, みずしか のまない) — no がしか, no をしか. When the amount feels like not-enough, Japanese reaches for しか…ない almost every time.
      · ケンしか てつだってくれない。  —  Ken is the only one who'll help me.
      · ひとりしか いない。  —  There's only one person.
      · せんえんしか ない。  —  I've only got a thousand yen.
   NOT: ケンしか てつだってくれる。 — しか has no meaning without its negative — the verb must be ない-shaped: ケンしか てつだってくれない. With a positive verb, use だけ: ケンだけ(が) てつだってくれる.
