# m20 — Comparisons I: のほうが…より

Module 20 · Comparing

Saying one thing beats another. Japanese leads with the WINNER — 「でんしゃの ほうが バスより たかい」 — with ほうが on the one that wins and より on the one that loses, and the adjective never changes shape, because Japanese has no comparative ending at all. Ask with 「AとBと どっちが〜？」 (どちら to a stranger), and pay for it with the numbers and counters a real comparison needs: ひゃく, せん, まん and the generic 〜こ.

## Lesson 1 — 「でんしゃの ほうが たかい」 — the winner goes first (ja-m20-neo-1)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「でんしゃの ほうが たかい」 — the winner goes first
   Japanese leads with the WINNER. 「Aの ほうが Bより 〜」 means A is more ~ than B: ほう marks the one that wins and より hangs the one that loses off the back of it. English does the opposite — it leads with the comparison word, 'more expensive', 'cheaper', 'bigger' — and here is the good news: Japanese has no such word and no comparative ending. **Neither ほう nor より changes the adjective.** たかい is たかい whether you are comparing or not, and there is nothing extra to learn. ほう itself is an ordinary noun meaning 'the ~ side, the ~ one', which is why it takes の in front of it exactly like any other noun and が behind it: 「でんしゃの ほう」 is 'the train one'. より sits straight after the loser with nothing between them. Word order is loose and 「バスより でんしゃの ほうが たかい」 says the same thing, but lead with ほうが while you are learning, because that is the half nobody ever drops.
      · でんしゃの ほうが バスより たかい。  —  The train is more expensive than the bus.
      · この みせの ほうが やすい。  —  This shop is the cheaper one.
      · じてんしゃの ほうが くるまより やすい。  —  A bike is cheaper than a car.
      · すしの ほうが パンより おいしい。  —  Sushi is more delicious than bread.
      · ミカの ほうが ケンより いそがしい。  —  Mika is busier than Ken.
   NOT: でんしゃ ほうが バスより たかい。 — ほう is a NOUN, so whatever describes it needs の in front: でんしゃの ほうが バスより たかい。

## Lesson 2 — 「バスより やすい」 — より on its own (ja-m20-neo-2)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「バスより やすい」 — より on its own
   There is a shorter way to say the same thing, and it is the one you will hear. Put the winner up as the TOPIC with は, hang the loser off より, and stop: 「でんしゃは バスより たかい」. Same meaning as the ほうが version, one word lighter. は does here exactly what it has always done — it announces what the sentence is about — and より keeps its one job of marking the loser. In an ANSWER you can drop even more. Once both things are already on the table, 「バスより やすい」 on its own is a complete reply; nobody repeats the winner. That is the everyday shape of a comparison: ほうが…より is the frame you learn the pattern with, より alone is what comes out of your mouth. The one thing that never moves is より's position — directly after the loser, never in front of it — and the adjective still does not change.
      · でんしゃは バスより たかい。  —  The train is more expensive than the bus.
      · バスより やすい。  —  It's cheaper than the bus.
      · この かばんは あの かばんより おおきい。  —  This bag is bigger than that bag.
      · きょうは きのうより さむい。  —  Today is colder than yesterday.
      · すしは とりにくより おいしい。  —  Sushi is more delicious than chicken.
   NOT: でんしゃは バス たかい。 — the loser needs より on it — with no particle at all, バス just collides with the adjective: でんしゃは バスより たかい。

## Lesson 3 — 「どっちが たかい？」 — asking which of two (ja-m20-neo-3)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「どっちが たかい？」 — asking which of two
   To ASK a comparison, name the two things with と and finish with どっち: 「でんしゃと バスと どっちが たかい？」 — of the train and the bus, which is the more expensive one? と is the same 'and' you have used for a closed list since module 8, and that is exactly the job it is doing: the second と marks the list as FINISHED, so the listener knows there are two options and only two. Say it while you are learning even though speech often drops it. どっち takes が and never は, because a question word cannot be the thing a sentence is ABOUT. The answer comes back with ほうが — 「でんしゃの ほうが たかい」 — and needs no より at all, because the question already named both things. どっち is the plain, everyday word. Its polite twin is どちら: same meaning, same slot, and the only difference is who you are talking to.
      · でんしゃと バスと どっちが たかい？  —  Which is more expensive, the train or the bus?
      · すしと とりにくと どっちが すき？  —  Which do you like more, sushi or chicken?
      · どっちが やすい？  —  Which is the cheaper one?
      · この かさと あの かさと どっちが あたらしい？  —  Which umbrella is newer, this one or that one?
      · えいがと おんがくと どちらが すきですか。  —  Which do you prefer, films or music? — the same question, said politely
   NOT: でんしゃと バスと どっちは たかい？ — a question word can never be the topic, so どっち always takes が: でんしゃと バスと どっちが たかい？

## Lesson 5 — 「さんびゃくえん」 — hundreds, and the three that change sound (ja-m20-neo-4)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「さんびゃくえん」 — hundreds, and the three that change sound
   Hundreds are a number plus ひゃく, and most of them are as plain as they look: yonhyaku (400), gohyaku (500), nanahyaku (700), kyuuhyaku (900) — the number goes in front, the counter does not budge. Three of them DO budge, and those three are the whole lesson. 300 is sanBYAKU, 600 is roPPYAKU, 800 is haPPYAKU: the h softens to b after san, and it hardens to p — with the sound in front of it doubling — after roku and hachi. Nothing else in the hundreds changes. Say the three out loud a few times, because the mouth learns this far faster than the eye does, and a hundred you have to stop and reconstruct is a hundred you cannot use at a till. Building a bigger number is then just stacking, exactly as it was for 11-99: 「ごひゃく」 is 500, and 500 yen is 「ごひゃくえん」 with えん straight on the end.
      · この ほんは さんびゃくえんだ。  —  This book is 300 yen.
      · きっぷは ろっぴゃくえんだ。  —  The ticket is 600 yen.
      · この かさは はっぴゃくえんだ。  —  This umbrella is 800 yen.
      · この ぼうしは ごひゃくえんだ。  —  This hat is 500 yen.
      · あの めがねは きゅうひゃくえんだ。  —  Those glasses are 900 yen.
   NOT: この ほんは さんひゃくえんだ。 — 300 is さんびゃく — さん turns ひゃく into びゃく. Only 400, 500, 700 and 900 leave the counter alone: この ほんは さんびゃくえんだ。

## Lesson 6 — 「いくら？」 — thousands, and what things cost (ja-m20-neo-5)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「いくら？」 — thousands, and what things cost
   Past nine hundred you need two more words and both of them behave. せん is a thousand and まん is ten thousand, and they stack the same way the hundreds did: yonsen (4,000), gosen (5,000), nanasen (7,000), kyuusen (9,000), and ichiman for 10,000 — ten thousand always carries its ichi, unlike sen and hyaku, which stand alone. ## Lesson 7 — 「たまごが さんこ」 — counting small things (ja-m20-neo-6)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「たまごが さんこ」 — counting small things
   〜こ is the everyday counter for a small object you can pick up — a key, a piece of fruit, a box, an egg. When you do not know the specialised counter for something small, this is the one that will not be wrong. Attach it straight to the number: sanko (3), yonko (4), goko (5), nanako (7), kyuuko (9). Four cells double the sound in front of them and are worth saying out loud until they stick: ikko (1), rokko (6), hakko (8), jukko (10). Now the part that catches English speakers, and it is not the counter at all — it is where the count SITS. English puts 'three' in front of 'keys'. Japanese does not: the noun comes first with が or は on it, and the number-plus-counter stands on its own just before the verb. 「かぎが さんこ ある」 — as for keys, three exist. And a bare number can never do this job by itself; without a counter on it there is nothing to attach the count to the sentence with.
      · かぎが さんこ ある。  —  There are three keys.

## Lesson 9 — 「としょかんの ほうが しずかだ」 — な-adjectives compare too (ja-m20-neo-7)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「としょかんの ほうが しずかだ」 — な-adjectives compare too
   な-adjectives compare exactly like い-adjectives, and this is where the promise that nothing changes earns its keep. Drop one into the frame and nothing in it moves: 「としょかんの ほうが きっさてんより しずかだ」. What a な-adjective still needs is its だ, because it is not a verb and cannot end a sentence on its own — しずか is a quality, しずかだ is a statement. ほう and より do not affect that in either direction: they are not verbs, they are not endings, and they leave the predicate exactly as it would have been in a sentence with no comparison in it at all. The same goes for すき and きらい, which are な-adjectives even though English hears them as verbs: 「すしの ほうが すきだ」 is 'sushi is the one I like more'.
      · としょかんの ほうが きっさてんより しずかだ。  —  The library is quieter than the coffee shop.

## Lesson 10 — 「どっちも たかいけど」 — both are, but one is more (ja-m20-neo-8)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「どっちも たかいけど」 — both are, but one is more
   Often both things are true and you still have a preference, and Japanese has a tidy way to say so. Put も on BOTH halves and you get 'both of them': 「でんしゃも バスも たかい」 — the train and the bus are both expensive. も is the same 'too' you have had since module 3; doubling it is what turns two separate 'too's into one pair. Then hang the comparison off the end with けど: 「でんしゃも バスも たかいけど でんしゃの ほうが たかい」. That is the shape of a real opinion — concede first, choose second — and it is far friendlier than flatly saying one thing is worse. When both things have already been named you can shorten the first half to どっちも, 'both of them': 「どっちも たかい」.

## Lesson 11 — 「どちらが やすいですか」 — asking a stranger which (ja-m20-neo-9)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「どちらが やすいですか」 — asking a stranger which
   どちら is どっち's polite twin. Same meaning, same slot in the sentence, and the only thing that changes is who is standing in front of you: with a friend it is どっち, with a shop assistant or a stranger it is どちら. When you reach for どちら the rest of the sentence goes polite with it, because a polite question word in a plain sentence sounds like two people talking: 「どちらが やすいですか。」 Watch the か. A casual question rides its rising contour alone — 「どっちが やすい？」 — but a polite question REQUIRES か on the end, and without it you have said a statement and left it hanging. Everything else stays exactly where it was: と…と still ties the pair, ほうが still marks the winner in the answer, and より still marks the loser.
      · どちらが やすいですか。  —  Which one is cheaper?
      · この かばんと あの かばんと どちらが おおきいですか。  —  Which bag is bigger, this one or that one?
      · こちらの ほうが あたらしいです。  —  This one is the newer one.
      · すみません この とけいは いくらですか。  —  Excuse me, how much is this watch?
      · この ほんの ほうが たかいですか。  —  Is this book the more expensive one?
   NOT: どちらが やすいです。 — a polite question REQUIRES か — without it the sentence is a statement, and an unfinished one: どちらが やすいですか。
