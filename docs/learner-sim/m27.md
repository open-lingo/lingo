# m27 — Explaining

Module 27 · Explaining

「ねつが あるんだ」 — the thing is, I've got a fever. Two characters on the end of a sentence you can already say turn a fact into an explanation, and んです is the very same item said politely. Then two shapes for what goes wrong and what changes: すぎる for too much of something (たべすぎた, たかすぎる) and く/に なる for a state that is turning into another one (さむく なる, いしゃに なる) — with なった for the change you have already noticed.

## Lesson 1 — 「ねつが あるんだ」 — saying it as an explanation (ja-m27-neo-1)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「ねつが あるんだ」 — saying it as an explanation
   There is a difference between stating a fact and explaining yourself, and Japanese marks it with two characters on the end. 「ねつが ある」 is a fact: I have a fever. 「ねつが あるんだ」 is the same fact offered as the REASON for something — why I am not coming, why I look like this, why you found me at home. Nothing about the sentence changes except that ending. Take the plain sentence you would have said anyway and put んだ on it: いく becomes いくんだ, たかい becomes たかいんだ. The negative and the past come along untouched — いかないんだ, のんだんだ — because んだ attaches to the finished sentence, not to the verb. That is the whole formation, and it is why this is easier than it looks. What takes practice is knowing WHEN. Use んだ when the sentence answers a question, asked or unasked: someone looks at your face and you say 「びょうきなんだ」; someone asks where you are going and you say 「びょういんに いくんだ」. Do not use it to announce a fact out of nowhere — 「あめが ふるんだ」 said to a stranger with no context sounds like the end of an argument nobody started. And one warning worth having early: a verb or an い-adjective takes ん and then だ. Never だ on its own.
      · ねつが あるんだ。  —  The thing is, I've got a fever.
      · きょうは いかないんだ。  —  I'm not going today, you see.
      · この じゅぎょうは むずかしいんだ。  —  The thing is, this class is difficult.
      · あしたは いそがしいんだ。  —  It's because I'm busy tomorrow.
      · どうして いかないんだ？  —  Why aren't you going?
   NOT: ねつが あるだ。 — a verb takes ん before だ — だ on its own finishes a NOUN and can never sit on a verb: ねつが あるんだ。

## Lesson 2 — 「びょうきなんだ」 — nouns put な in first (ja-m27-neo-2)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「びょうきなんだ」 — nouns put な in first
   When the thing you are explaining ends in a NOUN or a な-adjective, one small piece appears that catches everybody out. A noun sentence needs its copula — 「びょうきだ」, not 「びょうき」 — and in front of ん that copula changes shape: だ becomes な. So 「びょうきだ」 explaining itself is 「びょうきなんだ」. The だ has not disappeared and it has not doubled; it has changed clothes. な-adjectives behave identically, because they are nouns wearing a hat: しずかだ becomes しずかなんだ, すきだ becomes すきなんだ. And nouns of every kind take it — 「ぼくの かさなんだ」, it's my umbrella; 「あしたは やすみなんだ」, tomorrow is a holiday. Now the trap, and it is worth saying out loud. い-adjectives do NOT take な. たかい is already a whole predicate with no copula in it, so it goes straight to んだ: 「たかいんだ」, never 「たかいなんだ」. The rule of thumb is short: if you would have said だ, say な instead. If you would not have, do not add one.
      · びょうきなんだ。  —  The thing is, I'm ill.
      · ぼくの かさなんだ。  —  It's my umbrella, you see.
      · としょかんは しずかなんだ。  —  It's because the library is quiet.
      · さかなが すきなんだ。  —  The thing is, I like fish.
      · あしたは やすみなんだ。  —  It's a holiday tomorrow, you see.
   NOT: びょうきんだ。 — a noun still needs its copula before ん, and in front of ん だ becomes な: びょうきなんだ。

## Lesson 3 — 「どうして いかないんだ？」 — asking for the reason and giving it (ja-m27-neo-3)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「どうして いかないんだ？」 — asking for the reason and giving it
   から and んだ do two different jobs, and once you see the difference you will use them together constantly. から gives the reason INSIDE the sentence: 「ねつが あるから いかない」 — I have a fever, so I'm not going. んだ presents the WHOLE sentence as an explanation of something outside it. Put both in and you get the shape people actually use: 「ねつが あるから いかないんだ」. The から clause says why I'm not going; the んだ says that this is the answer to the question you were about to ask. Note where the んだ sits — on the very end, after the main clause. It never lands on the から clause in the middle. Asking is the mirror image. Put どうして at the front and keep the んだ at the back: 「どうして たべないんだ？」 That ん is what makes the question sound curious instead of blunt — a bare 「どうして たべない？」 can land like an accusation, while 「どうして たべないんだ？」 asks for the story. The answer comes straight back in the same shape, and the shortest useful answer of all is a reason with んだ on it: 「びょうきなんだ」.
      · ねつが あるから いかないんだ。  —  I'm not going, because I've got a fever.
      · どうして たべないんだ？  —  Why aren't you eating?
      · びょうきだから びょういんに いくんだ。  —  I'm going to the hospital because I'm ill.
      · おかねが ないから かわないんだ。  —  I'm not buying it, because I haven't got the money.
      · どうして この くつを かうんだ？  —  Why are you buying these shoes?
   NOT: ねつが あるからんだ いかない。 — んだ goes on the END of the whole sentence, never on the から clause in the middle: ねつが あるから いかないんだ。

## Lesson 5 — 「いかないんです」 — the same thing, said politely (ja-m27-neo-5)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「いかないんです」 — the same thing, said politely
   んだ and んです are ONE item in two skins. Same job, same attachment point, same な after a noun — only the last piece changes. 「いかないんだ」 to a friend; 「いかないんです」 to a teacher, a shop assistant, a doctor. Everything you learned in the last four lessons transfers whole. There are two things to get right and beginners drop one of them every time. First: the clause in FRONT of ん stays PLAIN in both skins. It is 「いかないんです」, never 「いきませんです」 — the politeness is carried once, on the very end, and the ます-form has no business in front of ん. Second: a polite QUESTION still needs か. 「どうして いかないんですか。」 Leave the か off and you have made a polite statement and left it hanging; leave the です off and you have asked a friend, not a stranger. A casual question needs neither, because its rising contour does the work: 「どうして いかない？」 The noun rule survives the change of skin intact: 「びょうきです」 explaining itself is 「びょうきなんです」, with the same な that 「びょうきなんだ」 has. This is the skin you want whenever you are explaining yourself to somebody you owe politeness to, which — outside your own friends — is nearly everybody.
      · どうして いかないんですか。  —  Why aren't you going?
      · ねつが あるんです。  —  I've got a fever, you see.
      · びょうきなんです。  —  The thing is, I'm ill.
      · きょうは じかんが ないんです。  —  The thing is, I haven't got time today.
      · どうして いかないんだ？  —  Why aren't you going? — the same question, said to a friend
   NOT: どうして いかないんです。 — a polite QUESTION requires か — without it the sentence is a statement, and an unfinished one: どうして いかないんですか。

## Lesson 6 — 「たべすぎた」 — more than you should have (ja-m27-neo-6)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「たべすぎた」 — more than you should have
   すぎる says that something went PAST the point where it should have stopped. Not a lot of it — too much of it. To build it, cut the verb back to the piece that sits in front of ます and put すぎる there instead: たべる gives たべ, のむ gives のみ, はたらく gives はたらき, あるく gives あるき. 「たべすぎる」 is eat too much. You have met that piece before: it is the same stem that carried 「たべに いく」 back in module 19, so finding it is not a new skill. すぎる is itself an ordinary る-verb, which means everything you know about る-verbs applies. The past is すぎた, and that is the form you will use most — too much is nearly always something you notice afterwards: 「たべすぎた」, I ate too much. Hang んだ on it and you have an apology ready-made: 「たべすぎたんだ」. One thing すぎる is not: it is not ちょっと and it is not よく. 「よく たべる」 says you eat a lot and means it kindly; 「たべすぎる」 says you eat more than you should and carries the regret with it. And すぎる never stands alone — there is always a stem in front of it, because on its own it names nothing.

## Lesson 7 — 「たかすぎる」 — too expensive to buy (ja-m27-neo-7)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「たかすぎる」 — too expensive to buy
   An adjective joins すぎる exactly the way a verb does: cut off the piece that ends it, and put すぎる there. For an い-adjective the piece to cut is the い. たかい gives たかすぎる, おおきい gives おおきすぎる, さむい gives さむすぎる, むずかしい gives むずかしすぎる. For a な-adjective there is nothing to cut at all, because it never had an ending — しずか goes straight to しずかすぎる. Now the part that surprises people. What comes out is a VERB, not an adjective. That means it never takes だ: 「たかすぎるだ」 is wrong for the same reason 「たべるだ」 was wrong in module 5. It also means it takes んだ directly, with no な in between: 「むずかしすぎるんだ」. 「たかすぎる」 is the single most useful sentence in a shop, and it says something 「よく たかい」 could never say. A price you merely dislike is たかい. たかすぎる is a decision about the purchase: too expensive FOR WHAT YOU WANTED TO DO. That is why すぎる so often comes with a から clause hanging off it — too expensive, so I'm not buying it.
      · この くつは たかすぎる。  —  These shoes are too expensive.

## Lesson 9 — 「だんだん さむく なる」 — the weather is changing (ja-m27-neo-9)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「だんだん さむく なる」 — the weather is changing
   なる is become, and everything interesting about it is in the piece that comes just before. For an い-adjective, swap the final い for く and put なる after it: さむい gives さむく なる, it gets cold. You have seen that く before — it is the shape an い-adjective takes whenever something follows it, which is why the negative was さむくない back in module 12. Nothing else changes, and the adjective itself is never touched again. What the sentence describes is a CHANGE, and that is the whole reason to reach for it. 「かみが みじかい」 says the paper is short. 「かみが みじかく なる」 says it is GETTING short — a different claim, and usually the more interesting one. Put だんだん in front when the change is gradual: 「だんだん さむく なる」. なる is an ordinary verb, so it does everything ordinary verbs do — んだ on the end to explain yourself, から to hang a consequence off it, けど to concede one. And いい is irregular here in exactly the way it was irregular in module 12: its く-form is よく, never いく, so get better is 「よく なる」.

## Lesson 10 — 「いしゃに なる」 — what you turn into (ja-m27-neo-10)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「いしゃに なる」 — what you turn into
   な-adjectives and NOUNS take に in front of なる, and that is the entire difference. げんきだ gives げんきに なる, しずかだ gives しずかに なる, いしゃだ gives いしゃに なる. Think of it as one slot with two fillers: く for い-adjectives, に for everything else. There is no だ anywhere in the result, because the に is already doing that work. The noun case is how Japanese says what somebody becomes, and it is worth practising on its own: 「いしゃに なる」, become a doctor; 「せんせいに なる」, become a teacher. English hides a verb in there — become, turn into, get to be — and Japanese does not: the noun, に, なる, done. Time words take it too. 「なつに なる」 is summer comes; 「はるに なる」 is spring arrives. That is why a weather forecast will say 「あたたかく なる」 for the temperature and 「はるに なる」 for the season in the same breath — two fillers, one slot. And because すき and きらい are な-adjectives, a change of taste rides the same frame: 「さかなが すきに なる」, to come to like fish.
      · げんきに なる。  —  I'll get better.

      · なつに なるから やまに いく。  —  Summer is coming, so I'm going to the mountains.
      · ばんは がっこうが しずかに なるんだ。  —  The thing is, the school goes quiet in the evening.
      · さかなが すきに なる。  —  I'm coming to like fish.
   NOT: げんきく なる。 — な-adjectives and nouns take に in front of なる, never く — く belongs to い-adjectives: げんきに なる。

## Lesson 11 — 「さむく なったんだ」 — reporting what has changed (ja-m27-neo-11)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「さむく なったんだ」 — reporting what has changed
   The past of なる is なった, and it is the form a change is usually reported in — because a change is something you notice after it has already happened. 「さむく なった」 is it's got cold. 「げんきに なった」 is he's better now. English often reaches for a present tense here (it IS cold now, he IS well now) and Japanese reaches for the past, because the news is the change rather than the state. Pair it with んだ and you have the most useful sentence shape in this module: 「かみが ながく なったんだ」, the thing is, the paper's got longer. Note what な does and does not do. なった is a VERB, so it takes ん straight after it — なったんだ, never なったなんだ. The な in なんだ only ever follows a noun or a な-adjective, and なった is neither. Two more things worth keeping straight. なった is the past of なる and not of ある: 「あった」 is a different verb with a different job. And a から clause in front of it puts the whole change in the past too — 「ふゆに なったから さむく なった」 — which is how you tell somebody what has changed and why, in one breath.
      · さむく なったんだ。  —  The thing is, it's got cold.
      · あには げんきに なった。  —  My older brother has got better.
      · ふゆに なったから さむすぎるんだ。  —  Winter's here, so it's too cold — that's the thing.
      · かみが ながく なったんです。  —  The thing is, the paper's got longer.
      · あねは せんせいに なった。  —  My older sister became a teacher.
   NOT: さむく なったなんだ。 — なった is a verb, so it takes ん straight after it — な belongs only after a noun or a な-adjective: さむく なったんだ。
