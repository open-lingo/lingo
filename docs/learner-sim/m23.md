# m23 — Experience & intent

Module 23 · Travel

「にほんに いった ことが ある」 — I have been to Japan, ever, at some point. That is not the past tense: it says the EXPERIENCE exists, so it is built from こと, が and ある, and 「ことが ない」 is how you say never. 「いく つもりだ」 is the other half — a plan you have made, hanging off the plain dictionary form, and 「いきたいけど いく つもりは ない」 is why wanting and deciding are different words. Then the clauses get harder: 「いく とき」 against 「いった とき」, 〜てから against 〜まえに, and a whole plan quoted under と おもう. Reading set 2 lands along the way: 山 川 海 上 下 小さい 足 来る.

## Lesson 1 — 「にほんに いった ことが ある」 — ever, not yesterday (ja-m23-neo-1)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「にほんに いった ことが ある」 — ever, not yesterday
   「にほんに いった」 means I went to Japan — one trip, on a date, and if you say it people will ask you when. 「にほんに いった ことが ある」 means something else entirely: I have been to Japan, at some point, ever. This is NOT a second past tense. It is a statement that the EXPERIENCE exists. Look at what it is made of and the whole thing falls out: こと is the act, the doing-of-it, so 「いった こと」 is the act of having gone; が marks it as the thing that exists; and ある is the same verb that has been putting things in rooms since module 6. The act of having gone EXISTS. Because こと is a thing and not a person, the verb is always ある and never いる. Three rules and you own it. The verb in front of こと is the PLAIN PAST — いった, たべた, みた, のんだ. The が never changes to は unless you are contrasting. And you never say when: the moment you add a date the sentence stops being about experience and goes back to being ordinary past. 「がいこくで はたらいた ことが ある」 — I have worked abroad. Whether it was last year or ten years ago is not what the sentence is for.
      · にほんに いった ことが ある。  —  I have been to Japan.
      · すしを たべた ことが ある。  —  I have eaten sushi.
      · にほんで はたらいた ことが ある。  —  I have worked in Japan.
      · あの えいがを みた ことが あるけど おもしろくなかった。  —  I have seen that film, but it wasn't interesting.
      · なつやすみに うみに いった ことが ある。  —  I have been to the sea in the summer holidays.
   NOT: にほんに いった ことが いる。 — こと is a THING, not a person or an animal — a thing exists with ある: にほんに いった ことが ある。

## Lesson 2 — 「のった ことが ない」 — never, and have you ever (ja-m23-neo-2)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「のった ことが ない」 — never, and have you ever
   The other half arrives free. If the experience EXISTS with ある, then it fails to exist with ない — 「ひこうきに のった ことが ない」, I have never been on a plane. That is the whole negative: no ません, no ませんでした, nothing about the past at all, because the sentence was never in the past to begin with. It says the act does not exist among the things you have done. ない is an adjective here, exactly as it was in module 6, so nothing follows it — 「ことが ないだ」 is not a sentence. Asking is just as cheap: raise your voice at the end. 「ふねに のった ことが ある？」 — have you ever been on a boat? Answer it with うん or ううん and the bare frame: 「ううん、ない」. And the two halves love sitting in one sentence, because that is how people actually talk about themselves: 「おさけを のんだ ことが ないけど すしは たべた ことが ある」 — I have never drunk sake, but I have eaten sushi. Note the は on すし there: the moment you contrast two experiences, the second one takes the topic particle.
      · ひこうきに のった ことが ない。  —  I have never been on a plane.
      · うみで およいだ ことが ない。  —  I have never swum in the sea.
      · ふねに のった ことが ある？  —  Have you ever been on a boat?
      · おさけを のんだ ことが ないけど すしは たべた ことが ある。  —  I have never drunk sake, but I have eaten sushi.
      · ちかてつに のった ことが ないから でんしゃで いく。  —  I have never taken the subway, so I'm going by train.
   NOT: ひこうきに のった ことが ないだ。 — ない is an ADJECTIVE and finishes the sentence by itself — nothing follows it: ひこうきに のった ことが ない。

## Lesson 3 — 「いく つもりだ」 — the plan you have made (ja-m23-neo-3)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「いく つもりだ」 — the plan you have made
   つもり is a DECISION. 「なつやすみに にほんに いく つもりだ」 is not I will go and not I want to go — it is I intend to go, I have made up my mind, the plan is made. That is why it is a noun: つもり literally is your intention, and だ finishes it the way だ finishes any noun. The verb in front is the PLAIN DICTIONARY form and nothing else. いく つもり, かう つもり, まつ つもり. はいる — to enter, to go in — takes the same slot: 「みせに はいる つもりだ」, I intend to go into the shop. Never ます, never たい, never the past — an intention is about something that has not happened yet, so 「いきます つもり」 is wrong in a way a Japanese speaker hears immediately. Everything else behaves normally: hang a reason on から, a contrast on けど, and put the time wherever time goes. 「りょこうに いく つもりだけど じかんが ない」 — I mean to travel, but I have no time, which is a sentence you will want more often than you would like. To ask, drop the だ and let your voice rise: 「なつやすみは なにを する つもり？」 — what are you planning to do in the summer?
      · なつやすみに にほんに いく つもりだ。  —  I intend to go to Japan in the summer holidays.
      · あした きっぷを かう つもりだ。  —  I'm planning to buy a ticket tomorrow.
      · ミカと えいがを みる つもりだ。  —  I intend to see a film with Mika.
      · りょこうに いく つもりだけど じかんが ない。  —  I intend to travel, but I have no time.
      · あさ ちかてつに のる つもりだ。  —  I'm planning to take the subway in the morning.
   NOT: なつやすみに にほんに いきます つもりだ。 — つもり hangs off the plain DICTIONARY form, never the polite ます-form: なつやすみに にほんに いく つもりだ。

## Lesson 5 — 「いきたいけど いく つもりは ない」 — wanting is not deciding (ja-m23-neo-5)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「いきたいけど いく つもりは ない」 — wanting is not deciding
   たい and つもり are not the same word twice. たい (module 13) says what you WANT: 「にほんに いきたい」 — I want to go to Japan, and whether you ever will is left completely open. つもり says what you have DECIDED: 「にほんに いく つもりだ」 — the plan exists. Wanting is a feeling; intending is a plan; and the four combinations are all real. You can want to and intend to — 「にほんに いきたいから いく つもりだ」. The place this lesson keeps reaching for is くうこう, the airport: 「くうこうに いきたいから きっぷを かう つもりだ」, I want to go to the airport, so I intend to buy a ticket. You can want to and NOT intend to, which is the most useful of the four and the one English fumbles: 「にほんに いきたいけど いく つもりは ない」, I'd love to go, but I'm not going to. You can intend to without wanting to, which is what work is. And you can do neither. Notice the negative there: to say you have no intention you do not negate the verb, you say the intention itself does not exist — 「つもりは ない」, with は because it is a contrast. That is the same ある/ない you just used for experience, one lesson later, doing the same job on a different noun.
      · にほんに いきたいから いく つもりだ。  —  I want to go to Japan, so I intend to.
      · すしを たべたいけど たべた ことが ない。  —  I want to eat sushi, but I never have.

## Lesson 6 — 「のまない つもりだ」 — deciding NOT to (ja-m23-neo-6)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「のまない つもりだ」 — deciding NOT to
   An intention not to do something is built the obvious way and it is worth doing once slowly, because learners reach for the wrong negative here. You do NOT negate つもり. You negate the VERB and leave つもり alone: 「きょうは おさけを のまない つもりだ」 — I intend not to drink today. The ない-form is module 6's, unchanged: のむ → のまない, いく → いかない, たべる → たべない, みる → みない, およぐ → およがない. Put it straight in front of つもり and finish with だ. Keep it apart from the other negative you met one lesson ago. 「いかない つもりだ」 is I intend not to go — a decision. 「いく つもりは ない」 is I have no intention of going — no decision was ever made. English blurs them; Japanese does not, and the difference is exactly where the ない sits. Everything else is ordinary: 「あした がっこうに いかない つもりだから うちで まつ」 hangs a reason on から like any other clause.

## Lesson 7 — 山, 川, 海 — reading set 2 (ja-m23-neo-7)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 山, 川, 海 — reading set 2
   Second reading set. The ladder has not changed since module 18 — a kanji appears, you say what it says, and you are never asked to write one. What is worth adding now is WHY a glyph can sound like two different things. Japanese borrowed the characters from Chinese and kept both sounds: the native Japanese word that already meant the thing, and an approximation of the Chinese one. ## Lesson 9 — 「いく とき」 と 「いった とき」 — which side of the when (ja-m23-neo-9)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「いく とき」 と 「いった とき」 — which side of the when
   とき is module 15's word, and here is the half module 15 held back. In English the clause before when takes the tense of the real world: when I go to Japan, when I went to Japan. In Japanese the tense inside a とき clause is measured against the MAIN clause, not against now — and that one difference decides which form you use. 「にほんに いく とき ひこうきに のる」: the getting on the plane happens BEFORE the arriving, so the clause is non-past, いく. 「にほんに いった とき すしを たべた」: the sushi happens AFTER the arriving, so the clause is past, いった. Both sentences may be about last year and both may be about next year — the tense of いく/いった is not telling you when, it is telling you which side of the とき you are standing on. Test it on yourself: 「りょこうに いく とき カメラを かう」 buys the camera first; 「りょこうに いった とき カメラを かった」 buys it once you are there. The clause is ALWAYS plain — ます never appears in front of とき — and a noun joins with の: 「がくせいの とき」, back when I was a student.
      · にほんに いく とき ひこうきに のる。  —  When I go to Japan, I take a plane.
      · にほんに いった とき すしを たべた。  —  When I got to Japan, I ate sushi.
      · りょこうに いく とき カメラを かう。  —  When I go travelling, I buy a camera first.
      · うみに いった とき およいだ。  —  When I got to the sea, I went swimming.
      · がくせいの とき にほんで はたらいた ことが ある。  —  I have worked in Japan, back when I was a student.

## Lesson 10 — 「かってから のる」 — putting a trip in order (ja-m23-neo-10)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「かってから のる」 — putting a trip in order
   An itinerary is a list of things in order, and Japanese has two ways to say which comes first. 〜てから is after doing: take the て-form, add から, and the rest of the sentence happens next. 「きっぷを かってから でんしゃに のる」 — I'll buy a ticket and THEN get on the train. This から is not the から that means because; it is the から that has meant from since module 16, and 「かってから」 is literally from having bought. The other direction is まえに, which you already own: 「ひこうきに のる まえに コーヒーを のむ」 — before getting on the plane, I drink a coffee. Notice what changes and what does not. Before まえに the verb is always the plain DICTIONARY form no matter when any of it happens; before から it is always the て-form. Neither one takes a past. The order lives entirely in the connector, and the て-form is the one that has to be derived: かう → かって, たべる → たべて, およぐ → およいで, まつ → まって.
      · きっぷを かってから でんしゃに のる。  —  I'll buy a ticket, then get on the train.
      · すしを たべてから ホテルに はいる。  —  I'll eat sushi, then go into the hotel.
      · うみで およいでから ちゃを のむ。  —  I'll swim in the sea, then drink some tea.
      · はたらいてから がいこくに いく つもりだ。  —  I intend to work first, then go abroad.
      · ひこうきに のる まえに コーヒーを のむ。  —  Before getting on the plane, I drink a coffee.
   NOT: きっぷを かうてから でんしゃに のる。 — から attaches to the て-form, and the て-form of かう is かって — かうて is not a word: きっぷを かってから でんしゃに のる。

## Lesson 11 — 「いく つもりだと おもう」 — quoting a whole plan (ja-m23-neo-11)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「いく つもりだと おもう」 — quoting a whole plan
   Everything this module built is a CLAUSE, which means it can be quoted. Module 18 taught the frame: a plain sentence, と, then おもう or いう. Feed it a whole つもり sentence and you get 「ミカは にほんに いく つもりだと おもう」 — I think Mika is planning to go to Japan. Feed it an experience and you get 「トムは すしを たべた ことが あると おもう」 — I think Tom has eaten sushi. Nothing new is being learned here; what is new is that the thing you are quoting is now two clauses deep, because 「いく つもりだ」 already had a verb inside a noun inside a copula. The rules do not bend for the extra depth. What sits in front of と is ALWAYS plain, so it is つもりだと, never つもりですと. The negative works the same way: 「ひこうきに のった ことが ないと おもう」. And いう swaps in for おもう when you are reporting speech rather than opinion — 「あしたは いかない つもりだと ミカに いう」, I'll tell Mika I'm not planning to go tomorrow, with に marking the person the words go to.
      · ミカは にほんに いく つもりだと おもう。  —  I think Mika is planning to go to Japan.
      · トムは すしを たべた ことが あると おもう。  —  I think Tom has eaten sushi.
      · ケンは ひこうきに のった ことが ないと おもう。  —  I don't think Ken has ever been on a plane.
      · あしたは いかない つもりだと ミカに いう。  —  I'll tell Mika I'm not planning to go tomorrow.
      · この ホテルは たかいと おもうけど はいる つもりだ。  —  I think this hotel is expensive, but I intend to go in.
   NOT: ミカは にほんに いく つもりですと おもう。 — what goes in front of と is ALWAYS plain — です never appears there: ミカは にほんに いく つもりだと おもう。
