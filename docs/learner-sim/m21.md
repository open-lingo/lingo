# m21 — Listing & describing: や, たり

Module 21 · Listing

Two words for ‘and’, and the difference is whether you have finished. 「いぬと ねこが いる」 names every animal there is; 「いぬや ねこが いる」 names two of them and leaves the rest unsaid, with など to say so out loud. 〜たり 〜たり する does the same for what you DO. Family II arrives beside it — おかあさん / おとうさん for other people’s families against m17’s はは / ちち for your own — and the cup counter 〜はい (いっぱい, さんばい, ろっぱい) counts the drinks you order.

## Lesson 1 — 「うみや やまが すきだ」 — the list that isn’t finished (ja-m21-neo-1)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「うみや やまが すきだ」 — the list that isn't finished
   English has one word for 'and' and Japanese has two, and the difference is whether the list is FINISHED. や says it is not. 「うみや やまが すきだ」 means the sea and the mountains — and other things you have not bothered to name. It is the everyday shape: nobody lists their whole life, they name two examples and let や carry the rest. Put や between the items, and give the LAST item the particle the sentence actually needs — が, を, に, whatever the verb wants. や never replaces that particle, it only joins. If you want to be explicit that the list is open, put など on the end of the last item: 「ほんや じしょなどが ある」, books and a dictionary and so on. That is the whole of it. The one thing to fight is the English instinct to hear や as 'and' and stop there, because the next lesson gives you と, which means 'and' too — and says the opposite about the list.
      · うみや やまが すきだ。  —  I like the sea and the mountains, among other things.
      · あさは パンや たまごを たべる。  —  In the morning I eat bread and eggs and things like that.
      · かばんに ほんや じしょなどが ある。  —  In my bag there are books, a dictionary and so on.
      · いけや かわで あそぶ。  —  I play at ponds and rivers and places like that.
      · ももや きゅうりを かう。  —  I'll buy peaches and cucumbers and a few other things.
   NOT: うみや やまや すきだ。 — や goes BETWEEN the things you are listing — the last one still needs the particle the sentence wants, here が: うみや やまが すきだ。

## Lesson 2 — 「いぬと ねこが いる」 — the list that is (ja-m21-neo-2)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「いぬと ねこが いる」 — the list that is
   と is the other 'and', and it closes the list. 「いぬと ねこが いる」 means there is a dog and a cat and that is all of them — you have named everything. 「いぬや ねこが いる」 means a dog, a cat, and other animals besides. Same English, opposite claim, and the only difference on the page is one kana. Use と when the list is genuinely complete: the two things in your bag, the two people who came, the whole shopping list. Use や when you are giving examples. Both sit BETWEEN the nouns and neither one replaces the particle at the end — 「パンと たまごを かう」 keeps its を exactly as the や version did. One more job worth remembering, because you have had it since module 8: と also means 'with' when it sits before a person and a verb — 「ミカと えいがを みる」 is watching a film with Mika, not a list of two things.
      · いぬと ねこが いる。  —  There's a dog and a cat — those two, and that's the whole list.
      · いぬや ねこが いる。  —  There's a dog and a cat and other animals too — や leaves the list open.
      · パンと たまごを かう。  —  I'll buy bread and eggs — the whole shopping list.
      · ミカと えいがを みる。  —  I'm watching a film with Mika — と also means 'with'.
      · じしょと えんぴつが かばんに ある。  —  The dictionary and the pencil are in my bag.
   NOT: いぬ ねこが いる。 — two nouns cannot just sit next to each other — a finished list needs と between them: いぬと ねこが いる。

## Lesson 3 — 「ミカの おかあさん」 — the other family words (ja-m21-neo-3)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「ミカの おかあさん」 — the other family words
   Japanese has two words for every family member, and which one you use depends on WHOSE family it is. はは, ちち, あに, あね — the words from module 17 — are for YOUR OWN family when you talk about them to somebody else. They are humble: you lower your own side. おかあさん, おとうさん, おにいさん, おねえさん are the honorific set, and they are for EVERYBODY ELSE'S family. 「ミカの おかあさん」 is Mika's mother; 「ミカの はは」 is simply wrong, because はは is a word you only ever use about your own. There is one more place the honorific set turns up, and it surprises people: you use it to your own parents' FACES. 「おかあさん ちゃが ある？」 — Mum, is there any tea? Talking TO her, she is おかあさん; talking ABOUT her to an outsider, she is はは. So the question is never 'which word means mother'. Both do. The question is whose family, and who is listening.
      · ミカの おかあさんは せんせいだ。  —  Mika's mother is a teacher.
      · ちちは かいしゃで はたらく。  —  My father works at a company — ちち is how you speak about your OWN father to other people.
      · ケンの おとうさんも かいしゃで はたらく。  —  Ken's father works at a company too.
      · ともだちの おかあさんは にほんじんだ。  —  My friend's mother is Japanese.
      · おかあさん ちゃが ある？  —  Mum, is there any tea? — おかあさん is also what you call your own mother to her face.
   NOT: ミカの ははは せんせいだ。 — はは is only ever YOUR OWN mother — somebody else's mother is おかあさん: ミカの おかあさんは せんせいだ。

## Lesson 5 — 「ミカの おにいさん」 — の decides whose (ja-m21-neo-4)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「ミカの おにいさん」 — の decides whose
   の is what makes the family words safe, and it has been doing this job since module 4: it says WHOSE. 「ミカの おにいさん」 is Mika's older brother. 「あに」 with nothing in front of it is already 'my older brother' — the humble word carries the 'my' inside it, which is why it needs no の and why you must never hang somebody else's name on it. So the two sets divide cleanly. Somebody else's family: a name or a person plus の plus the honorific word — ともだちの おねえさん, せんせいの おかあさん. Your own family: the humble word on its own — あに, あね, ちち, はは. If you want to be explicit you can say わたしの あに or うちの ちち, and people do, but the plain word already means it. Get this one habit and the whole two-word system stops being two words to remember and becomes one question to ask: whose?
      · ミカの おにいさんは がくせいだ。  —  Mika's older brother is a student.
      · あには だいがくに いく。  —  My older brother goes to university — あに already means 'my'.
      · ともだちの おねえさんは かいしゃで はたらく。  —  My friend's older sister works at a company.
      · あねは えいがが すきだ。  —  My older sister likes films.
      · せんせいの おねえさんも せんせいだ。  —  The teacher's older sister is a teacher too.
   NOT: ミカの あには がくせいだ。 — あに already means MY older brother, so it can never take somebody else's name — Mika's is おにいさん: ミカの おにいさんは がくせいだ。

## Lesson 6 — 「コーヒーを さんばい」 — counting what’s in the cup (ja-m21-neo-5)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「コーヒーを さんばい」 — counting what's in the cup
   〜はい counts what is IN a cup or a glass, not the cup itself: cupfuls of coffee, glasses of water, bowls of soup. It sits where every counter sits — after the thing, just before the verb: 「コーヒーを さんばい のむ」. The counter changes shape more than most, and the changes are the lesson. One is ippai. Three is sanBAI. Six is roPPAI. Ten is juPPAI. Everything else keeps the plain はい: yonhai (4), gohai (5), nanahai (7), kyuuhai (9) — and eight is haPPAI. Say the four odd ones out loud until your mouth does them without thinking, because a number you have to stop and rebuild is a number you cannot order a drink with. To ask, use なんばい: 「のみものを なんばい のむ？」 — how many drinks are you having? And the trap that catches every English speaker is not the sound change at all: a bare number cannot count a noun in Japanese, so 「コーヒーを さん のむ」 is not a slightly odd sentence, it is not a sentence.
      · コーヒーを さんばい のむ。  —  I drink three cups of coffee.
      · あさは みずを いっぱい のむ。  —  In the morning I drink a glass of water.

      · コーヒーが すきだから きょうも さんばい のんだ。  —  I like coffee, so I had three cups today as well.
   NOT: コーヒーを さん のむ。 — a bare number cannot count anything in Japanese — a cupful takes 〜はい, and after さん it turns into ばい: コーヒーを さんばい のむ。

## Lesson 7 — 「さんにんで よんはい」 — people take にん, cupfuls take はい (ja-m21-neo-6)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「さんにんで よんはい」 — people take にん, cupfuls take はい
   A counter is only really learned once you have to CHOOSE one, so here is 〜にん back beside 〜はい. にん counts people and はい counts cupfuls, and they are never interchangeable: 「かぞくは ごにんだ」 is five people, 「みずを ごはい のんだ」 is five glasses. Two people is ふたり and one is ひとり — the two irregulars from module 17 — and everything from three up is just the number plus にん: さんにん, よにん, ごにん. There is one more thing にん does that is worth having, because it turns up constantly around a table: put で on the number and it means 'as a group of that many'. 「ふたりで きっさてんに いく」 is the two of us going to the coffee shop. 「さんにんで コーヒーを よんはい のんだ」 — the three of us drank four coffees between us, and you can see both counters doing their own job in one sentence.

## Lesson 9 — 「みたり きいたり する」 — listing what you do (ja-m21-neo-7)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「みたり きいたり する」 — listing what you do
   や opens a list of things; 〜たり 〜たり する opens a list of ACTIONS. It is built out of something you already have: the plain past. Take たべた, add り — たべたり. Take のんだ, add り — のんだり. Do that to two verbs, line them up, and finish the sentence with する: 「あさは パンを たべたり ぎゅうにゅうを のんだり する」. In the mornings I do things like eat bread and drink milk. The 'things like' is not decoration — it is the whole point. You are naming a couple of examples out of many, exactly as や does, and a listener understands there was more. Two rules keep it upright. The たり pieces never change, however long the sentence gets. And する at the end is not optional: it is the verb of the sentence, and without it you have listed two half-actions and stopped. One more thing follows from that. Because する is the verb, する is what carries the tense — change it and the whole list moves with it: 「やすみは えいがを みたり おんがくを きいたり しました」, on my days off I DID things like watch films and listen to music. The たり pieces still do not move. That past form is the one you will hear most, because it is the answer to the question people actually ask: what did you do at the weekend?
      · やすみは えいがを みたり おんがくを きいたり する。  —  On my days off I do things like watch films and listen to music.
      · あさは パンを たべたり ぎゅうにゅうを のんだり する。  —  In the mornings I do things like eat bread and drink milk.
      · きっさてんで コーヒーを のんだり ほんを みたり する。  —  At the coffee shop I do things like drink coffee and look at a book.
      · やすみは すしを たべたり えいがを みたり する。  —  On my days off I do things like eat sushi and watch films.

## Lesson 10 — 「えいがや おんがくが すきだ」 — listing what you like (ja-m21-neo-8)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「えいがや おんがくが すきだ」 — listing what you like
   An open list and a preference belong together, because almost nobody likes exactly one thing. すき and きらい take が on the thing — they are adjectives, not verbs, however hard English fights you on it — and が is the particle the LAST item of a や list hands over to: 「あねは えいがや おんがくが すきだ」. My sister likes films and music and that sort of thing. Every piece of that is already yours: や joins, が marks what is liked, だ finishes the な-adjective. The same works with a たり list when what you like is an activity rather than a thing, and there the nominalizer の from module 13 does the joining instead: 「うみに いったり やまで あそんだり するのが すきだ」. And the negative is the same shape with きらい in place of すき — the thing you dislike still takes が, never を.
      · あねは えいがや おんがくが すきだ。  —  My older sister likes films and music and things like that.
      · ぼくは きのこが きらいだ。  —  I don't like mushrooms.
      · やすみは うみに いったり やまで あそんだり する。  —  On my days off I do things like go to the sea and play in the mountains.

## Lesson 11 — 「コーヒーを いっぱい ください」 — ordering (ja-m21-neo-9)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「コーヒーを いっぱい ください」 — ordering
   This is where the cup counter earns its keep. To order, name the thing with を, say how many, and finish with ください: 「コーヒーを いっぱい ください」 — one coffee, please. The count goes in the same slot it always does, straight before the verb, and ください IS the verb of the sentence. Drop the number and you still have a perfectly good order — 「みずを ください」 — so the counter is there to be precise, not to be grammatical. You can order a closed pair with と (「おさけと みずを ください」, sake and water, that's everything) or an open one with や, and the difference lands exactly as it did in lesson 1: と is your whole order, や invites a suggestion. ください is polite by itself, which is why it works with a stranger behind a counter with nothing else added; put すみません in front of it to get their attention first.
      · コーヒーを いっぱい ください。  —  One coffee, please.
      · みずを ください。  —  Some water, please.
      · ちゃを よんはい ください。  —  Four teas, please.
      · おさけと みずを ください。  —  Sake and water, please — と makes that the whole order.
      · すみません のみものを ください。  —  Excuse me — could we have some drinks.
   NOT: コーヒーが いっぱい ください。 — what you are ASKING FOR takes を — が would make the coffee the thing doing something: コーヒーを いっぱい ください。
