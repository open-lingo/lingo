# m28 — Must & should

Module 28 · Must & should

「いかなきゃ」 — gotta go. Japanese says you must do something by saying that not doing it won't do, and the second half gets swallowed: なきゃ and なくちゃ with friends, なければ ならない written down, なければ なりません to anybody you owe politeness to. Then the softer thing beside it — 「くすりを のんだ ほうが いい」, you'd better take medicine — where the た is not a past tense at all and the negative side is just ない. Eight compound kanji close the module: 会社 · 時間 · 電話 · 話す · 学校 · 先生 · 天気 · 外国.

## Lesson 1 — 「いかなきゃ」 — the thing you have to do (ja-m28-neo-1)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「いかなきゃ」 — the thing you have to do
   Japanese has no word for must. What it has is a sentence that says the other thing is not an option, and the useful half of it is short. Take the ない-form you have had since module 6 — 「いかない」, I'm not going — and swap that ない for なきゃ. いかない gives いかなきゃ, のまない gives のまなきゃ, しない gives しなきゃ, たべない gives たべなきゃ. Nothing else in the sentence moves: the topic, the object and the particles all stay exactly where they were, so 「きょうは かいしゃに いかない」 becomes 「きょうは かいしゃに いかなきゃ」 and the only thing that changed is the end of the verb. What it MEANS is have to, and it is a duty rather than a suggestion — a train to catch, a fever to treat, an office expecting you. It is also the shape you will hear most, because it is short and it is spoken. There is a longer, more formal version underneath it, and the next lesson takes that apart; for now, notice that なきゃ ends the sentence on its own. Nothing follows it. 「もう かえらなきゃ。」 is a complete thing to say, and it is what somebody says to you when they are standing up to leave.
      · きょうは かいしゃに いかなきゃ。  —  I have to go to the office today.
      · じかんが ないから もう かえらなきゃ。  —  I have to go home now, because I haven't got time.
      · ねつが あるから くすりを のまなきゃ。  —  I have to take medicine, because I've got a fever.
      · あしたも しごとを しなきゃ。  —  I have to do the work tomorrow as well.
      · うちに かえらなきゃ。  —  I have to go home.
   NOT: きょうは かいしゃに いくなきゃ。 — なきゃ replaces the ない of the ない-form, so it can never sit on a dictionary form: きょうは かいしゃに いかなきゃ。

## Lesson 2 — 「いかなければ ならない」 — what なきゃ is short for (ja-m28-neo-2)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「いかなければ ならない」 — what なきゃ is short for
   なきゃ is a contraction, and this is what it is a contraction of. Start from the ない-form again, but this time treat that ない as the い-adjective it behaves like: drop the い and put ければ there instead. いかない gives いかなければ, のまない gives のまなければ, しない gives しなければ. On its own that half means if I don't go — a condition hanging in the air with nothing to land on. What lands on it is ならない, which is it won't do. 「いかなければ ならない」 is therefore if I don't go it won't do, which is how Japanese says I have to go. Two things follow from that, and they are the whole lesson. First, ならない is not optional in careful speech, so the full form is two pieces and not one. Second, なきゃ already ATE it — that is why nothing can follow なきゃ, and why 「いかなきゃ ならない」 says the same thing twice. There is one more contraction worth recognising. なくては is a second conditional doing the same job, and it contracts to なくちゃ: 「いかなくちゃ」 means exactly what 「いかなきゃ」 means, in exactly the same register. Which one a speaker reaches for is habit, not grammar. Obligations are usually routines, so まいにち — every day — sits in front of one exactly where あした does: 「まいにち かいしゃに いかなければ ならない」, I have to go to the office every day.
      · あした かいしゃに いかなければ ならない。  —  I have to go to the office tomorrow.
      · ねつが あるから くすりを のまなければ ならない。  —  I have to take medicine, because I've got a fever.
      · じかんが ないから かえらなければ ならない。  —  I have to go home, because I haven't got time.

      · じかんが ないんだ。もう いかなくちゃ。  —  I haven't got time, you see. I have to go now.

## Lesson 3 — 「おかねが ないから はたらかなきゃ」 — the reason you have to (ja-m28-neo-3)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「おかねが ないから はたらかなきゃ」 — the reason you have to
   An obligation almost never travels alone. Somebody says they have to do something and you immediately want to know why, so the reason comes with it — and から is the particle that carries one, exactly as it did in module 16. The reason clause comes FIRST, から goes on its end, and the duty comes last: 「おかねが ないから まいにち はたらかなきゃ。」 The order matters and it is the reverse of English, which puts because in the middle. Think of it as the reason building up to the thing it forces. Every must-form works in that slot without changing: なきゃ, なくちゃ and the full なければ ならない all sit happily at the end of a から sentence. And the reason itself can be anything you can already say — a fever, a lack of money, a class tomorrow, a shop that closes. Two extras worth having now. まえに still does its module-15 job in front of a duty: 「だいがくに はいる まえに ことばを おぼえなきゃ。」 And a duty is a perfectly good answer to どうして, which means the shortest useful exchange in this module is two lines long: why aren't you coming — I have to work.
      · おかねが ないから まいにち はたらかなきゃ。  —  I have to work every day, because I haven't got the money.
      · あした じゅぎょうが あるから ことばを おぼえなきゃ。  —  I have to learn the words, because I've got a class tomorrow.
      · わからないから せんせいに きかなくちゃ。  —  I have to ask the teacher, because I don't understand.
      · びょうきだから やさいを たべなきゃ。  —  I have to eat vegetables, because I'm ill.
      · だいがくに はいる まえに ことばを おぼえなきゃ。  —  I have to learn the words before I get into university.
   NOT: おかねが ない まいにち はたらかなきゃ。 — the reason clause needs から on its end — without it the two halves are just two sentences pushed together: おかねが ないから まいにち はたらかなきゃ。

## Lesson 5 — 「いかなければ なりません」 — saying it to a teacher (ja-m28-neo-5)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「いかなければ なりません」 — saying it to a teacher
   なきゃ is what you say to a friend. This is what you say to everybody else. The polite pole of the same rule swaps ならない for なりません, and that is the only change: 「あした びょういんに いかなければ なりません。」 Everything in front of なりません stays PLAIN — the なければ half never becomes いきませんければ, which is not a thing, because politeness in Japanese is carried once and it is carried at the end. That is the same lesson module 27's んです taught with a different word on it. Now the part that catches people. なきゃ and なくちゃ have already swallowed the ending, so there is nothing left to make polite — there is no polite なきゃ. If you need the polite register you have to go back to the full なければ and finish it with なりません. This is why the long form is worth having even though the short one is what you hear: the long one is the only one that can be dressed up. Where does each belong? なきゃ and なくちゃ with friends, with family, and in your own head. なければ なりません with a teacher, at a counter, on the phone to a company, and in anything written down.

## Lesson 6 — 「くすりを のんだ ほうが いい」 — the better move (ja-m28-neo-6)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「くすりを のんだ ほうが いい」 — the better move
   Having spent four lessons on duties, here is the softer thing beside them: advice. 「くすりを のんだ ほうが いい」 is you'd better take medicine — not a rule, a recommendation, and the strongest one Japanese makes without being rude. You have most of it already. ほう is the ordinary noun from module 20, the ~ side, the ~ one; が marks it as what the sentence is about; いい is good. So the sentence literally says the having-taken-medicine side is good, and the alternative is quietly the loser of a comparison you never had to state. Because ほう is a NOUN, it needs が after it — dropping the particle leaves two nouns stacked with nothing between them. What goes in FRONT of ほう is the surprising bit, and it is the た-form: のんだ, たべた, いった, きいた, みた. Yes, the past tense. No, it is not about the past — the next lesson is entirely about why. For now, build it and hear it: 「やさいを たべた ほうが いい。」 「がっこうに いった ほうが いい。」 Advice comes with its reason as often as a duty does, so から is usually hanging off the front of it — and one of the reasons you will reach for most is つよい, strong, powerful: 「この くすりは つよいから いしゃに きいた ほうが いい」, this medicine is strong, so you'd better ask a doctor.

## Lesson 7 — 「たべない ほうが いい」 — the た that is not a past (ja-m28-neo-7)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「たべない ほうが いい」 — the た that is not a past
   The た in 「のんだ ほうが いい」 is the plain past you learned in module 11, and it is doing something it does nowhere else: nothing. It is not about yesterday. 「くすりを のんだ ほうが いい」 is advice about the next ten minutes. Japanese is picturing the situation in which the thing has already been done and calling THAT the better side, which is why the finished form of the verb turns up in a sentence about what to do next. English does something similar in you'd better have eaten first, and then loses its nerve. Here is the consequence that matters, and it is the half learners get wrong every time. The NEGATIVE side of this advice does NOT take a past. It takes the plain ない-form, unchanged: 「ねつが あるから いかない ほうが いい。」 「この くすりは のまない ほうが いい。」 The symmetry your ear wants — a past for the positive and a past negative for the negative — is not there, because 〜なかった ほうが いい would be regret about something already done rather than advice about something not yet done. So: た for the thing you should do, ない for the thing you shouldn't, and both of them sitting in front of the same ほうが いい.
      · ねつが あるから いかない ほうが いい。  —  You'd better not go, because you've got a fever.
      · にくは たべない ほうが いい。  —  You'd better not eat meat.
      · この くすりは つよいから のまない ほうが いい。  —  This medicine is strong, so you'd better not take it.
      · たかいから かわない ほうが いい。  —  You'd better not buy it, because it's expensive.
      · あめが ふるから かわで およがない ほうが いい。  —  It's going to rain, so you'd better not swim in the river.
   NOT: ねつが あるから いかなかった ほうが いい。 — the negative side of this advice takes the plain ない-form, never a past negative — 〜なかった ほうが いい is regret about something already done: ねつが あるから いかない ほうが いい。

## Lesson 9 — 会社 · 時間 · 電話 — reading the compounds (ja-m28-neo-9)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 会社 · 時間 · 電話 — reading the compounds

When two kanji are pushed together into a single word they almost always both take their Chinese readings, and the result sounds nothing like either word standing alone. 山 is やま by itself and さん inside a compound; 人 is ひと by itself and にん inside one. ## Lesson 10 — 「バスより ちかてつの ほうが いい」 — choosing between two (ja-m28-neo-10)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「バスより ちかてつの ほうが いい」 — choosing between two
   ほう has been doing quiet work for two lessons and it is time to say where it came from. Module 20 taught 「AのほうがBより〜」: the winner takes ほうが and the loser takes より. 「バスより ちかてつの ほうが いい」 is the underground is better than the bus — and 「ちかてつに のった ほうが いい」, the advice you have been building, is the SAME sentence with the losing side left unsaid. That is why advice uses ほう at all. It is a comparison of two courses of action in which only the winner gets named. Once you see it, two things become easy. You can put the loser back in whenever you want to be explicit: 「バスより ちかてつの ほうが いい。」 And you can compare two things that are not actions at all, which is exactly module 20's frame: 「この さいふより あの さいふの ほうが やすい。」 Note where the particles sit, because it is the one thing that goes wrong. ほう is a noun, so a noun in front of it needs の. より goes straight onto the losing side with nothing in front of it. Neither word inflects, ever.
      · バスより ちかてつの ほうが いい。  —  The underground is better than the bus.
      · この くつより あの くつの ほうが やすい。  —  Those shoes over there are cheaper than these.
      · この くすりは あの くすりより つよいから のまない ほうが いい。  —  This medicine is stronger than that one, so you'd better not take it.
      · にくより さかなを たべた ほうが いい。  —  You'd better eat fish rather than meat.
      · バスは うるさいから ちかてつの ほうが いい。  —  The bus is noisy, so the underground is better.
   NOT: バス ちかてつの ほうが いい。 — the losing side needs より on it — without the particle the two things are just sitting next to each other: バスより ちかてつの ほうが いい。

## Lesson 11 — 「あそびたいけど はたらかなきゃ」 — want against must (ja-m28-neo-11)

1. [grammar_rule] RULE CARD — 「あそびたいけど はたらかなきゃ」 — want against must
   Duties are only interesting because of what they are competing with, so here is the other side. 〜たい from module 13 says what you WANT: あそびたい, いきたい, たべたい, したい. Put it against a duty with けど in between and you have the most human sentence in this module: 「あそびたいけど はたらかなきゃ。」 I want to play, but I have to work. Two mechanical reminders, because both come back to bite. たい goes on the ます-stem — あそび, いき, たべ — never on the dictionary form, so it is あそびたい and never あそぶたい. And たい behaves as an い-adjective, which is why けど attaches straight to it with nothing in between, and why the negative is たべたくない. The pattern generalises further than it looks. Want against advice: 「コーヒーを のみたいけど のまない ほうが いい。」 Want against a reason: 「がいこくに いきたいから ことばを おぼえなきゃ。」 And the order can flip, with the duty first and the wish trailing behind it. What you cannot do is state both as plain facts side by side and hope the listener notices the tension — けど is what marks it.
      · あそびたいけど はたらかなきゃ。  —  I want to play, but I have to work.
      · うちに いきたいけど しごとを しなきゃ。  —  I want to go home, but I have to do the work.
      · がいこくに いきたいから ことばを おぼえなきゃ。  —  I want to go abroad, so I have to learn the words.
      · やさいを たべたくないけど たべなきゃ。  —  I don't want to eat vegetables, but I have to.
      · あそびたいけど あした じゅぎょうが あるんだ。  —  I want to play, but the thing is, I've got a class tomorrow.
   NOT: あそぶたいけど はたらかなきゃ。 — たい goes on the ます-stem, never on the dictionary form: あそびたいけど はたらかなきゃ。
