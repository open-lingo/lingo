## The structural model (what our explanations teach)

1. **Two-slot clause.** Every Japanese clause is a "something" plus a "happening/being": *A does B* or *A is B*. The engine — verb, adjective, or copula — closes its clause. Complex sentences are these two slots **nested**, never a new pattern to memorize.
2. **Modifiers precede.** Anything that modifies anything comes before it — including a whole clause modifying a noun. Nesting plus compounding is the entire growth story of a Japanese sentence; there is no third mechanism.
3. **が has one job.** が marks the doer/be-er of the engine — always. When no が is visible, the subject is still structurally present: a silent Ø-subject the listener fills from context. We surface that explicitly rather than calling sentences "subjectless" or giving が multiple "meanings."
4. **は is a topic flag, not a subject marker.** It sits outside the core clause and means "as for X." A card that says は marks the subject is a content defect, full stop.
5. **Helpers, not conjugation paradigms.** Verb "forms" are attachments to a stem: て links, いる adds ongoing state, ない negates, たい wants, ます politens. Teach the smallest formation once — that is the Conjugation Trainer's job — and let lessons reinforce **combinations** (ている is て + いる the learner already knows, not a fresh grammar point).
6. **Structure-true glosses.** Gloss a word the way Japanese uses it, not with the smoothest English. 自動詞 stay intransitive: 分かる ≈ "is clear / makes sense (to me)," never "I understand [it]." When natural English flips the structure, show the structural gloss first and the natural translation second.

## Authoring rules (test new content against these)

- **R1 — Decompose new shapes.** Introducing a sentence shape? Make the two slots visible (teach beat or build step) before drilling it.
- **R2 — One function per particle.** Never write "X sometimes means…". Apparent exceptions get explained by the model (topic flag, Ø-subject, helper attachment) — not listed as caveats.
- **R3 — Points are compositions.** The 13 te-compound/aux points (ている, てください, ことがある…) are authored as て + a piece the learner already has. Trainer teaches the piece; lessons teach the combination. (Concept-separation house rule, and the v2 trainer's framing.)
- **R4 — Gloss audit.** Any 自動詞 glossed as an English transitive is a defect. Queued sweep: grep vocab-card meanings for offenders (recon §F.3).
- **R5 — Just-in-time grammar.** Introduce a point where the content first needs it; never front-load theory the following steps don't exercise. The comprehensibility gate is the machine half of this rule.
- **R6 — Adult register.** Clean model over exception list. Never "Japanese is weird/hard here." If the model explains it, say so plainly; if it genuinely doesn't, admit that instead of hand-waving.
- **R7 — Own words, own examples.** Never reuse the educators' example sentences or phrasing. Fresh examples must pass the comprehensibility gate.

## What we deliberately do NOT import

- **"Immersion replaces the curriculum."** Lingo is an authored, SRS-scheduled course; the comprehensibility gate + trainers ARE our comprehensible-input delivery. Input-primacy is not an argument against authored drills here.
- **Anti-romaji absolutism.** Romaji fades on schedule (hiragana @ M10, katakana @ M17) by design — broader audience than either educator serves.
- **"Don't enumerate/schedule grammar."** Track B stays. We reconcile by framing points as compositions (R3), not by dropping SRS.
- **"Output is overrated."** Production practice (trainer Track B drills, the conversation-practice roadmap) is sequenced *after* input foundations — their point is about timing, and we honor it as timing, not as deletion.
