### Module intro mistranslates 見る as "reads" instead of "looks at"
module/lesson/step: m15 module header (text shown before Lesson 1)
severity: minor
effort: XS
confidence: verified
evidence: "Japanese has no relative pronoun — you park a whole sentence in front of a noun and the verb does not change: ほんを みる ひと is the person who reads books."
a native would say: ほんを みる ひと is "the person who looks at books" (見る = look at/glance, not read). If "reads books" is the intended meaning, a native writes ほんを よむ ひと — and よむ is already available (taught m1, drilled with conjugation in m11), so there's no vocabulary reason to avoid it here.
why it matters: this is the module's own framing sentence, stated before any exercise — it teaches learners a wrong equivalence for a basic verb (見る ≠ read) right at the point they're meant to trust it most, and it explains why every subsequent "person who looks at books" example in m15 (L1, L4, L8, L12, L13) reads as stilted to a native, since よむ was sitting there unused.

### Non-sequitur reason clause: liking cars is given as the reason for frequent shopping
module/lesson/step: m17 L1 step1 (grammar_rule) and step8 (build_sentence)
severity: minor
effort: S
confidence: verified
evidence: "ははは くるまが すきだから よく かいものに いく。 — My mother likes cars, so she often drives to go shopping."
a native would say: ちかくに スーパーが あるから よく かいものに いく or ひまだから よく かいものに いく — liking cars as a hobby doesn't causally explain shopping frequency, and the Japanese never mentions driving at all (the English gloss invents "drives" to paper over the gap).
why it matters: から/けど lessons are precisely where the causal logic of the example sentence is the whole point being drilled — a nonsensical cause-effect pair undermines the thing being taught, and the added "drives" in the English gloss doesn't correspond to any word in the Japanese.

### Non-sequitur reason clause: absence of snow given as reason for a summer beach trip
module/lesson/step: m16 L14 (Challenge) step13, dialogue_listen
severity: minor
effort: XS
confidence: verified
evidence: "Tanaka: なつやすみは どうでしたか。 Tom: ろくがつから くがつまで にほんに いきました。ゆきが ぜんぜん なかったので うみに いきました。"
a native would say: あつかったので/てんきが よかったので うみに いきました — nobody explains a beach trip during June-September by noting the absence of snow, since snow was never a possibility in Japan in summer to begin with.
why it matters: reads as a template artifact (ぜんぜん + negative practice bolted onto an unrelated context) rather than something a person would actually say, in the one slot (dialogue_listen) meant to model authentic speech.

### Register clash: plain then sudden ます within one dialogue turn to the same listener
module/lesson/step: m18 L13 (Challenge) step13, dialogue_listen
severity: major
effort: XS
confidence: verified
evidence: "Tanaka: この じゅぎょうは どうですか。 Ken: むずかしいと おもう。でも たのしいと おもう。あした きます。"
a native would say: keep the whole turn in one register when addressing the same person without a topic/stance shift — either むずかしいと おもいます。でも たのしいと おもいます。あした きます。 (consistently polite, appropriate since Tanaka addressed Ken with ですか) or drop きます to plain いく if the whole turn is meant to be casual.
why it matters: this is exactly the register-mixing pattern the course elsewhere teaches to avoid, and it appears in the same lesson that explicitly states "politeness lives on the FINAL verb of the sentence and nowhere else" — the example dialogue violates the lesson's own rule mid-turn.

### Students answer teacher Tanaka's polite questions in fully plain form, against the course's own established convention
module/lesson/step: m18 L2 step11 and L7 step11, dialogue_listen
severity: major
effort: S
confidence: verified
evidence: L2: "Tanaka: ミカさんは きょう きますか。 Ken: くると おもう。じゅぎょうが あるから くると おもう。" — L7: "Tanaka: ミカさんは もう たべましたか。 Tom: もう たべたと おもう。きっさてんに いったと おもう。"
a native would say: くると おもいます／じゅぎょうが あるから くると おもいます (and similarly おもいます for L7) — matching how the same character (Tanaka) is answered everywhere else in the course: m16 L3 (ません), m16 L9 (ませんでした), m17 L7 (です), m19 L4/L7/L9/L11/L13 (all ます/です), where a student consistently returns politeness to a teacher who addressed them formally.
why it matters: this is a genuine character/register inconsistency, not just an isolated stylistic choice — the same teacher figure who is answered politely eight other times across m16/m17/m19 is answered in bare plain form twice in m18, right when the lesson is teaching that politeness rides on the final verb only, making the lapse most visible here.

### Character-name collision: Mika describes her own friend as also being named "Mika"
module/lesson/step: m18 L12 (Review) step12, dialogue_listen
severity: minor
effort: XS
confidence: verified
evidence: "Tom: あの ひとは なんと いう なまえ？ Mika: ミカと いう。わたしの ともだちだ。" — Q1 correct answer: "She's called Mika and she's her friend."
a native would say: give the friend a different name (e.g. さくらと いう。わたしの ともだちだ。) — reusing the speaking character's own established name for a separate, unrelated NPC in the same line is confusing and reads as an authoring slip, not a deliberate choice (there's no joke or plot point made of the coincidence).
why it matters: recurring named characters (Mika, Tom, Ken, Tanaka) are the course's whole cast — collapsing two of them into the same name breaks the illusion that these are consistent people the learner is tracking across modules.

### Over-explicit わたしは repeated on both sides of a direct reply
module/lesson/step: m14 L3 step11, dialogue_listen
severity: minor
effort: XS
confidence: verified
evidence: "Tom: ミカは どこに すんでいる？ Mika: わたしは しずかな いえに すんでいる。トムは？ Tom: わたしは にぎやかな いえに すんでいる。"
a native would say: しずかな いえに すんでいる。トムは？ ／ にぎやかな いえに すんでいる。 — once the question is direct and personal ("where do you live"), the reply's subject is obvious from context; a native drops わたしは in both turns, especially in the second turn where Tom is answering the very question Mika just bounced back to him.
why it matters: repeating the subject pronoun on every line of a back-and-forth is a textbook-ese tic that no natural conversation has — this is the kind of over-explicit subject flagged as a category to watch for, not the already-filed missing-register-cue issue.

### Weak/implausible causal link used as a drilled example (secondary instance)
module/lesson/step: m17 L6 step5, listening_comprehension
severity: minor
effort: S
confidence: suspected
evidence: "AUDIO: ちちは ごじゅうさんさいだから よく およぐ — Q: My father is fifty-three, so he swims often."
a native would say: this one is plausible (retirees/health-conscious 50s often take up swimming) but is weaker than the module's own べつの pairing "ちちは ごじゅうさんさいだけど げんきだ" (age framed as contrast with vigor) — using から to assert that being 53 itself causes frequent swimming reads as a slightly forced justification rather than the more natural げんきだから よく およぐ.
why it matters: minor stylistic weakness rather than a clear defect — flagged as suspected/lower-confidence since a listener could reasonably supply the "and he's still fit" bridge themselves; included for completeness, lowest priority of this set.
